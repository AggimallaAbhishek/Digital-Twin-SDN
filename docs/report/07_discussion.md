# 7. Discussion and Limitations

## 7.1 What the results show

- **H1 is not shown.** Plain SDN (V1) did not recover in any of its nine runs, so every V1 time to recover is a lower bound and all three H1 verdicts are inconclusive. Beyond the recovery rule, the loop clearly helps when congestion comes from *load* that can be spread: in the flash crowd, both acting variants more than doubled the affected flows' throughput and cut their loss from 44% to under 10% (Section 6.2). It helps less, or harms, when the problem is not load. After an AP failure the stations had already moved by themselves, and in two of three V3 runs a failed rollback then cut one station off for the rest of the run. Under interference, the right action was a channel change, and V3 would not apply it without an operator.
- **The twin's main value is as a gate, not as a filter.** H2 holds narrowly. The twin rejected few actions (3 of V2's 64, 4 of V3's 65), and 2 of the 3 rejected ones that V2 applied did harm. But 38 of the rollbacks, and all three failed rollbacks, followed actions the twin had accepted. The twin predicts the steady state after a change, and only its throughput prediction was validated (within a few per cent, Section 6.3). The executor's watch measures the 30 s right after the change, transient included, on throughput, latency, loss and fairness. Validating the latency and loss predictions, and modelling the transient, would let the twin catch more. Until then the twin alone is not enough: the watch and rollback are what limited the harm.
- **Safety and availability pull against each other.** V3 kept its rule in every run: nothing high-impact without an operator. That is exactly why it could not repair the interference in an unattended campaign, while V2, which broke the rule nine times, recovered. With an operator in the loop, as in the live demo, the waiting channel change is one click away. The campaign measures the system with nobody at the console.
- **Rollback can do harm of its own.** In three AP-failure runs the loop had steered a station off ap2 shortly before ap2 failed. The watch blamed the failure on the steer, and the rollback tried to steer the station back onto the dead AP. The testbed disconnects a station before joining the target, so the station was left with no AP. Two fixes follow. A rollback should never target an AP that is down (fall back to the best AP still up). The watch should not judge an action while an outside event, such as an AP failure, is unfolding. Of all the results, this is the most important to fix before the system controls a real network.

What else the results show:

- **A simple, analytical twin is accurate enough to act on.** It predicts per-flow throughput within 4.5% (video within 4.5% in every case but a channel change), in well under a millisecond. Verifying a change before applying it costs nothing a person would notice, so there is no speed argument for acting without the twin.
- **The language model is useful as a translator and an explainer, not as a controller.** It translated 30 of 30 intents and explained 3 of 3 live faults, but it is never trusted directly: its output is validated, compiled by deterministic code into bounded actions, and checked in the twin.
- **A forecast did not help.** On this campus, load changes in steps (an AP fails, a crowd arrives), and trend extrapolation overshoots more often than it anticipates. Reacting to measured load, checked in the twin, proved the more reliable design.

## 7.2 Limitations

- **Virtual network.** Mininet-WiFi emulates the radio with a fixed propagation model, on one machine. Real radios add fading, interference from devices outside the network, and client behaviour the model does not have. The numbers are about this emulation, not about a real campus.
- **An analytical twin.** The twin's model is calibrated on this testbed. Bulk TCP flows are its weakest case (12–21% error), and it would need recalibrating for other hardware or traffic. It predicts the steady state after a change, not the transient while clients move.
- **A narrow action set.** The heuristics can steer clients, change a channel or transmit power, set QoS queues and rate limits, all within fixed bounds. When no allowed move is left, the loop does nothing. This happened in one of the three flash-crowd seeds of the loop batch: V3 steered 4 of 11 clients away and then had no target within the signal bound (decision P4.5-D).
- **An unattended evaluation.** The campaign ran with no operator, so V3 never applied a high-impact action (Section 6.2). Its results describe the system with nobody at the console, not with an operator approving changes as designed.
- **The twin does not predict the transient.** Most regressions the executor rolled back followed actions the twin had accepted (Section 6.3). It predicts the steady state, not a steer's first 30 s, and only its throughput prediction was validated against the testbed.
- **Steer rollback.** Undoing a steer means steering the clients back to their previous AP. When that AP has failed, the rollback fails and leaves the station without an AP, as in three AP-failure runs. The executor records the failure, but has no fallback.
- **Three seeds.** With three seeds per variant and scenario, the 95% intervals are wide ($t = 4.30$), and only large differences are distinguishable. Rare behaviours may not appear at all.
- **Censored recovery times.** A run that does not recover before it ends is counted at its run end. Means of time to recover are then lower bounds, and some H1 comparisons can only be called inconclusive (Section 6.1).
- **The local model is weaker.** Without internet the fallback model translated 21 of 30 intents and explained 2 of 9 replayed faults. The system stays safe, because invalid or unhelpful output is refused or harmless, but less useful offline.
- **One operator, one building.** Approvals, the dashboard and the copilot were designed for a single operator watching one campus.

## 7.3 Threats to validity

- **Internal.** The same team built the system and the evaluation. To limit this, the KPI definitions, the harm rule for H2 and the H1 verdict rule were fixed in recorded decisions (P4.5-A, P6.5-A, P6.6-A) *before* the campaign was run. The code was frozen (`eval-v1`) before the campaign, and every number in Chapter 6 is produced by a script from recorded data.
- **Construct.** "Recovered" combines utilisation under 80% with video p95 latency under 50 ms for 15 s. A different threshold would move the recovery times. The 50 ms figure is the project plan's video example, not a measured user requirement. "Harmful" means the executor measured a regression of more than 10% and rolled the action back. Smaller harms are not counted, which makes H2 conservative. In the other direction, the watch cannot tell an action's effect from an outside event in the same seconds: 4 of the 43 measured regressions had a scripted disruption inside their watch, so they may not be the action's doing (Section 6.3).
- **Conclusion.** Three seeds give low statistical power. Differences inside the confidence intervals are reported as not shown, not as no difference.
- **External.** One emulated campus layout, three scenario types and one traffic mix. Other layouts, more APs or real devices could behave differently. The variants differ only in the controller, so the comparison between them is fair, but the absolute numbers do not transfer.
