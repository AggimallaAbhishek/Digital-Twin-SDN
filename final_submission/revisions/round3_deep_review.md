# HIGH LEVEL SUMMARY

## Summary
This paper presents a twin-gated closed-loop control architecture for campus Wi-Fi Software-Defined Networking (SDN) in which candidate network optimization actions—whether generated by a heuristic load-balancing optimizer or compiled deterministically from natural-language operator intents translated by a large language model (LLM)—must pass a pre-deployment analytical digital twin verification gate before execution. The digital twin models per-access-point (AP) fluid max-min airtime sharing, distance-weighted co-channel interference, and calibrated $M/M/1/K$ priority queueing at sub-millisecond speeds ($0.61\text{ ms}$ median), while an executor monitors live network key performance indicators (KPIs) over a $30\text{ s}$ post-deployment watch window and automatically rolls back actions that trigger regressions exceeding $10\%$. Evaluated across $27$ emulated runs in Mininet-WiFi with a Ryu controller covering three disruption scenarios (lecture flash crowd, AP failure, and co-channel interference), the paper transparently reports both positive results—such as doubling affected flow throughput during flash crowds and achieving $30/30$ schema-validated intent translation with a cloud LLM—and rigorous negative empirical findings demonstrating that the steady-state analytical twin fails to filter out harmful actions in the closed loop ($2$ of $23$ rejected) and that the post-deployment watch-and-rollback mechanism provides the primary operational protection.

## Strengths
* **Rigorous and Transparent Reporting of Negative Empirical Results:** The authors provide an exceptionally candid and reproducible empirical analysis of what did not work, demonstrating that even when a steady-state analytical twin achieves $4.5\%$ mean absolute percentage error on offline per-flow throughput, its closed-loop predictions perform no better than a naive "nothing changes" baseline and fail to reject most harmful actions due to unmodeled client re-association transients and overlapping infrastructure faults.
* **Defense-in-Depth Architecture for LLM-Driven Network Automation:** The system design effectively isolates the LLM from direct network manipulation by enforcing JSON schema validation, deterministic policy-to-action compilation, hard parameter bounds, digital twin pre-verification, and a post-deployment watch-and-rollback executor, offering a practical engineering blueprint for safe intent-based wireless management.
* **Sub-Millisecond Analytical Twin and Reproducible Open Artifact:** The fluid airtime and $M/M/1/K$ surrogate executes in a median of $0.61\text{ ms}$ without neural training overhead, easily fitting within a $5\text{ s}$ control loop, and the submission is backed by a complete, automated reproducibility package (`make results`, `make paper`, and `make campaign`) with all recorded telemetry and action ledgers.

## Weaknesses
* **[W1] Confounded Ablation Design Between the Digital Twin Gate and Operator Approval Gate:** Between the direct-action baseline (V2) and the full system (V3), the experimental setup simultaneously introduces both the digital twin verification gate and an operator approval gate for high-impact actions in an unattended campaign where no operator ever approves queued actions. Because the co-channel interference scenario requires a high-impact channel change to restore capacity, V3 queues $8$ twin-accepted channel changes that are never applied and fails to recover, confounding the causal effect of the digital twin with that of an absent operator; adding an auto-approving V3 variant or an operator-gated V2 variant would isolate the twin's contribution.
* **[W2] Circular Co-Channel Contention Model and Simplified Analytical Surrogate:** Because Mininet-WiFi does not natively emulate co-channel contention in this setup, the testbed enforces the exact same load-independent distance-weighted capacity formula ($C_i$ in Eq. 1) as a traffic-control rate cap that the twin uses for prediction, validating software consistency rather than physical 802.11 contention dynamics and omitting CSMA/CA backoff and multi-rate anomalies (Reference Paper: Performance analysis of the IEEE 802.11 distributed coordination function, G. Bianchi, 2000; Reference Paper: Performance anomaly of 802.11b, M. Heusse, F. Rousseau, G. Berger-Sabbatel, and A. Duda, 2003). Furthermore, the single-class $M/M/1/K$ latency surrogate (Eq. 3) is mathematically capped at $15.8\text{ ms}$—below the $50\text{ ms}$ recovery threshold—and lacks separate offline validation for latency, loss, or transients, in contrast to learned multi-queue performance models that capture complex queueing delays (Reference Paper: RouteNet-Fermi: Network modeling with graph neural networks, M. Ferriol-Galmés, J. Paillisse, J. Suárez-Varela, K. Rusek, S. Xiao, X. Shi, X. Cheng, P. Barlet-Ros, and A. Cabellos-Aparicio, 2023).
* **[W3] Passive Baseline Selection, Right-Censoring, and Calibration Seed Reuse:** The closed-loop campaign reuses the exact same three random seeds ($42, 43, 44$) from the calibration dataset—introducing potential in-sample bias and precluding parametric confidence intervals—and compares against a passive plain SDN baseline (V1) in which stations never roam away from overloaded or interfered APs, leading to $100\%$ right-censoring at the $600\text{ s}$ horizon. Evaluating on fresh unseen seeds and comparing against standard client-side BSS transition roaming would better establish practical recovery gains (Reference Paper: IEEE Standard for Information Technology—Telecommunications and Information Exchange Between Systems—Local and Metropolitan Area Networks—Specific Requirements—Part 11: Wireless LAN Medium Access Control (MAC) and Physical Layer (PHY) Specifications, IEEE, 2021).
* **[W4] Unverified Rollbacks During Infrastructure Faults and Permissive Verifier Logic:** During the $30\text{ s}$ watch window, the executor cannot distinguish an exogenous scheduled AP failure at $240\text{ s}$ from an action-induced regression and executes rollbacks without re-checking hard safety bounds (S1) or AP liveness, rolling steered stations back onto a dead AP and contaminating both recovery metrics (H1) and twin harm-rate attributions (H2). In addition, the verifier acceptance rule (Eq. 6) uses a logical disjunction ($\lor$) where a $>5\%$ improvement in client-count fairness $J$ alone can bypass severe predicted regressions in throughput, latency, or loss, and sequential single-state verification in Algorithm 1 does not update the twin state across multiple proposals within the same tick.
* **[W5] Out-of-Loop LLM Evaluation and Silent Semantic Translation Misses:** While the multi-stage schema-and-verifier intent pipeline builds on established patterns in verifiable network configuration synthesis (Reference Paper: Making Network Configuration Human Friendly, C. Wang, M. Scazzariello, A. Farshin, D. Kostić, and M. Chiesa, 2023; Reference Paper: Verified Prompt Programming for Network Configuration, R. Mondal, A. Tang, R. Beckett, T. Millstein, and G. Varghese, 2023), Hypothesis H3 is evaluated entirely offline on a small internal set of $30$ unambiguous intents rather than in the closed loop or on broader SDN intent benchmarks such as NetConfEval or IBNBench (Reference Paper: NetConfEval: Can LLMs facilitate network configuration?, C. Wang, M. Scazzariello, A. Farshin, S. Ferlin, D. Kostić, and M. Chiesa, 2024; Reference Paper: NetIntent: Leveraging Large Language Models for End-to-End Intent-Based SDN Automation, M. K. Hossain and W. Aljoby, 2025). Moreover, all $9$ of the local model's failures on compound intents passed JSON schema validation and deterministic compilation, showing that the pipeline enforces syntactic and hard-bounds validity rather than semantic fidelity.

### [System Model, Analytical Twin Formulation, and Control Loop Methodology] (Pages: 2-4)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

* **Verification Summary of Analytical Derivations:** The closed-form analytical expressions in Section IV—specifically the piecewise-linear distance weight $w(d_{ij})$ in Eq. (1), the strict-priority max-min fair-share allocation in Eq. (2), the steady-state M/M/1/K mean system size $L(\rho, K)$ and its continuous limit $L(1, K) = K/2$ in Eq. 1. **[I2.1] Sequential Single-State Verification, Multi-Target Steering Bounds, and Concurrent Watch Attribution (Section IV-B, Section V & Algorithm 1):**
   A potential concern in the closed-loop execution design is an inconsistency between how multiple proposals within a single $5\text{ s}$ tick are generated, verified, and watched. Section IV-B formulates the safe control problem as selecting a *set* of actions $a_t$ at tick $t$ evaluated jointly via $\hat{\mathbf{k}} = f(s_t, a_t)$. However, in Algorithm 1 (Lines 5–14) and Section V (*Safety enforcement*), the twin state $s$ is built once at the start of the tick (Line 5), and the heuristic loop iterates over proposals $p \in P$, verifying each proposal individually against the unmodified initial tick state $s$ (`v <- VERIFY(s, p)` on Line 8) while allowing up to 3 accepted actions to be applied in the same tick, each starting its own concurrent $30\text{ s}$ watch:
   - *Unupdated Twin State and Potential (S1) Bound Violation:* In Section V, client steering is described as: "an AP at or above utilization 0.8 sheds up to 30% of its clients, rounded down, as one proposal per target AP; the targets are the APs below 0.6, least utilized first, and the stations moved are those with the strongest predicted signal at the target." Whenever two or more candidate target APs have utilization below 0.6, `PROPOSE(s)` generates multiple proposals in the same tick. It is unclear whether the $\lfloor 0.3 n_i \rfloor$ client budget and selected stations are partitioned across all eligible target APs or generated independently per target AP. Because Line 8 verifies each proposal $p \in P$ independently against the static initial state $s$ rather than updating the simulated state sequentially ($s \leftarrow \text{simulate\_state}(s, p)$) after earlier proposals in the same tick are accepted, verifying multiple proposals from the same overloaded source AP ignores the load reduction, queue changes, and station movements already committed by earlier proposals in that tick. Under the per-target interpretation, up to 3 proposals could each pass the 30% hard bound (S1) individually against $s$ and be applied in the same tick, either attempting to steer overlapping sets of high-signal stations to different target APs or cumulatively shedding 60–90% of the source AP's clients in one tick—violating (S1). Furthermore, if an overloaded AP has $n_i \le 3$ clients, $\lfloor 0.3 n_i \rfloor = 0$, leaving unspecified whether steering is disabled or clamped to 1 client.
   - *Watch Guard vs. Unattributable Concurrent Watches:* While Algorithm 1 (Lines 2–4) blocks proposals in *subsequent* ticks while a watch is active ("so effects are not mixed up"), applying up to 3 actions in the *same* tick creates concurrent $30\text{ s}$ watches whose flow scopes overlap (e.g., on the shared source AP and on network-wide fairness $J$). Section V notes that "a regression could not be attributed among them," and Algorithm 1 (Line 1) does not specify whether a KPI regression on a shared AP triggers a simultaneous rollback of all concurrent watches from that tick or how attribution is resolved. Sequentially updating the simulated state $s$ across accepted proposals within the tick (or verifying same-tick proposals jointly, as done for LLM policies) and restricting the heuristic loop to at most 1 applied action per tick so that Line 2 strictly serializes all watched actions would eliminate unmodeled intra-tick interactions and watch-attribution ambiguity.

2. **[I2.2] Unchecked Topology Constraints During Rollback (Condition S4, Algorithm 1 Line 1) and Stale State for Delayed Operator Approvals (Condition S3) (Section IV-B & Section V):**
   Two state-verification omissions in Section IV-B and Algorithm 1 represent substantive methodological vulnerabilities in dynamic network environments:
   - *Unverified Rollbacks Executed Before State Refresh (Condition S4):* In Algorithm 1, due watches and rollbacks are processed at Line 1—*before* the twin state $s$ is refreshed from the last $10\text{ s}$ of telemetry at Line 5—and Condition (S4) specifies that when a watched KPI regresses by more than 10% and exceeds its noise floor (ignoring the first $10\text{ s}$), "the action is rolled back, without checking (S1) again, so the rollback assumes the topology did not change during the watch." Simultaneously, Algorithm 1 (Lines 2–4) blocks all new state evaluations and proposals for the full $30\text{ s}$ watch duration. Because a watch spans $30\text{ s}$—six times longer than the $5\text{ s}$ silence threshold required for the twin to detect a downed AP (Section III-B)—an external AP failure occurring during an active watch causes the watch to conflate the infrastructure failure with an action-induced KPI regression and blindly steer stations back onto a dead AP (or an AP below the $-75\text{ dBm}$ signal threshold), while blocking the controller from reacting to the fault on the next $5\text{ s}$ tick. Refreshing the telemetry state $s$ at the very start of Algorithm 1, aborting active watches upon verifiable infrastructure events (such as an AP in the action's scope going down), and re-verifying hard bounds (S1) (specifically target AP operational liveness and signal constraints in the latest state $s_t$) prior to executing any rollback would make the safety enforcement self-consistent.
   - *Stale Verification for Operator-Approved Actions (Condition S3):* Condition (S3) states that "an approved action is applied as verified, not re-verified against a later state." Because human operator approval introduces an arbitrary delay across multiple $5\text{ s}$ ticks, the live network state at approval time may have drifted substantially from the state $s_t$ against which the proposal was originally verified. Re-running `VERIFY(s_current, p)` upon operator approval before execution is recommended to prevent applying stale high-impact actions.

3. **[I2.3] Permissive Logical Disjunction in the Verifier Acceptance Rule (Eq. 6), Client-Count Fairness Bypass ($J$, Eq. 4), and Zero-Baseline Sign Ambiguity (Section IV-A & Section IV-B):**
   A potential concern arises in the formulation of the verifier acceptance rule in Eq. (6) and its boundary definitions on pages 3–4:
   $$\text{accept} \iff \left(\forall k : \delta_k \ge -5\right) \lor \left(\exists k : \delta_k > 5\right).$$
   - *Safety Bypass via Disjunctive Acceptance and Unweighted Station Counts:* Because the non-regression clause and the improvement clause are connected by a logical disjunction ($\lor$) rather than a conjunction ($\land$), the second clause ($\exists k : \delta_k > 5$) completely overrides the first clause ($\forall k : \delta_k \ge -5$). Consequently, any candidate action predicted to improve even a single KPI by more than $5\%$ is unconditionally accepted by (S2), even if the twin simultaneously predicts severe regressions ($\delta_k \ll -5\%$) in throughput, latency, or packet loss. This vulnerability is compounded by including Jain's fairness index $J$ (Eq. 4) in the four-element KPI vector $\mathbf{k} = (T, L_{95}, \bar{\ell}, J)$. Because $J$ is computed solely over raw AP client association counts $n_i$—independent of heterogeneous flow demands $\lambda_f$, co-channel capacity reductions $C_i$ in Eq. (1), or signal quality—any client-steering action from a more populated AP to a less populated AP deterministically increases $J$ with zero prediction error, which can satisfy $\delta_J > 5$ by itself and bypass negative predictions on actual traffic KPIs. Although the authors transparently note this behavior on page 4, structuring the safety gate as a logical conjunction that requires bounded degradation across all performance KPIs ($\forall k \in \{T, L_{95}, \bar{\ell}\} : \delta_k \ge -\tau_{\text{deg}}$) alongside an improvement criterion would better align Eq. (6) with its role as a safety gate.
   - *Undefined Directionality for the $k = 0 \ne \hat{k}$ Edge Case:* The text at the top of page 4 specifies $\delta_k = 100(\hat{k} - k)/|k|$ for throughput and $J$, and $\delta_k = 100(k - \hat{k})/|k|$ for latency and loss, with the boundary rule "$\delta_k = 0$ if $k = \hat{k}$, and $\delta_k = \pm\infty$ if $k = 0 \ne \hat{k}$." Because the denominator $|k|$ is zero when $k = 0$, the sign of $\pm\infty$ cannot be determined from the ratio itself without explicitly stating the polarity rule. Since all four KPIs are non-negative, transitioning from $k = 0$ to $\hat{k} \ne 0$ implies $\hat{k} > 0$, which represents an improvement for higher-is-better metrics (throughput $T$ and fairness $J$, so $\delta_k = +\infty$) but a degradation for lower-is-better metrics (latency $L_{95}$ and loss $\bar{\ell}$, so $\delta_k = -\infty$). Moreover, under the disjunctive rule in Eq. (6), when baseline loss is zero ($k = 0$) and predicted loss is positive ($\hat{k} > 0$), $\delta_{\bar{\ell}} = -\infty$ falsifies the first clause $(\forall k : \delta_k \ge -5)$ but still allows the action to be accepted if another KPI improves by $>5\%$ (and a naive implementation assigning $+\infty$ whenever $k = 0 < \hat{k}$ would treat newly induced packet loss on a previously lossless flow as an infinite improvement). Explicitly writing $\delta_k = \text{sgn}(\hat{k} - k)\cdot\infty$ for throughput/$J$ and $\delta_k = \text{sgn}(k - \hat{k})\cdot\infty$ for latency/loss when $k = 0 \ne \hat{k}$ would eliminate this ambiguity.

4. **[I2.4] Structural Mismatch Between the Bounded M/M/1/K Latency Surrogate (Eq. 3), the Recovery Objective (Eq. 5), and Priority Queueing Dynamics (Section IV-A & Section IV-B):**
   In Section IV-A (page 3), queue latency is approximated using the mean number in a finite-buffer M/M/1/K queue in Eq. (3), $d_q = d_0 + s L(\rho_q, K)$, with calibrated parameters $d_0 = 0.8\text{ ms}$, $s = 2.5\text{ ms}$, and $K = 6$. While the algebraic expression for $L(\rho, K)$ and its continuous limit $L(1, K) = K/2$ are mathematically exact, three interconnected modeling concerns arise in relation to the recovery objective $T_{\text{rec}}$ in Eq. (5) ($U(\tau) < 0.8$ and $L_{95}(\tau) \le 50\text{ ms}$ for $15\text{ s}$) and the verifier rule in Eq. (6):
   - *Saturation Ceiling vs. Recovery Threshold and Verifier Sensitivity:* Because $L(\rho_q, K)$ is strictly bounded above by $K = 6$ as $\rho_q \to \infty$ (and equals $K/2 = 3$ at $\rho_q = 1$), the predicted latency $d_q$ has a hard upper bound of $\lim_{\rho_q \to \infty} d_q = d_0 + sK = 0.8 + 2.5 \times 6 = 15.8\text{ ms}$ (and equals $8.3\text{ ms}$ at $\rho_q = 1$). While the authors candidly note that this $15.8\text{ ms}$ cap prevents the twin from ever predicting a violation of the $50\text{ ms}$ recovery threshold in Eq. (5), it also directly impairs the verifier gate in Eq. (6): because the heuristic controller only proposes actions when an AP is overloaded ($U \ge 0.8$, where $\rho_q$ approaches or exceeds $1$), under heavy overload ($\rho_q \gg 1$) both the no-action baseline $k(s_t)$ and the post-action prediction $\hat{k}$ saturate near the $15.8\text{ ms}$ asymptote, compressing the relative latency change $\delta_{L_{95}}$ toward $0\%$ and rendering the verifier insensitive to severe queueing delay differences precisely when congestion is worst.
   - *Cumulative Priority Load in a Single-Class M/M/1/K Formula and Fixed Service Constant $s$:* In Eq. (3), the load for priority level $q$ is defined cumulatively as $\rho_q = \sum_{q' \preceq q} \sum_{f \in q'} \lambda_f / C_i$ and substituted directly into the single-class formula $L(\rho_q, K)$. Unlike Eq. (2), which explicitly subtracts capacity consumed by higher-priority queues ($R_q = \max(0, C_i - \sum_{q' \prec q} \sum_{f \in q'} x_f)$), plugging cumulative offered load into a single-class finite-buffer formula understates the waiting time experienced by lower-priority queues when higher-priority traffic consumes most of $C_i$. Moreover, by Little's Law, mean queueing delay in an M/M/1/K system scales inversely with effective service rate $C_i$ ($\propto L(\rho_q, K)/\lambda_{\text{eff}}$), whereas multiplying $L(\rho_q, K)$ by a fixed constant $s = 2.5\text{ ms}$ treats packet service time as invariant when co-channel interference reduces $C_i$ in Eq. (1).
   - *Station-Level Nearest-Rank Percentile vs. Packet Delay Distribution:* Defining $L_{95}$ in the twin as the nearest-rank 95th percentile across stations of the mean queue delay $d_q$ of each station's highest-priority queue (with 20 stations across 4 APs, and typically $\le 10$ stations on the source and target APs compared by a single action's verdict) means that $\hat{L}_{95}$ reduces to the maximum deterministic station mean delay in the action's scope, rather than modeling the 95th percentile of the packet delay distribution measured by live telemetry in Eq. (5).

5. **[I2.5] Circularity and Load-Independence of the Co-Channel Interference Model (Section III-A & Section IV-A, Eq. 1):**
   Equation (1) models the effective downlink capacity $C_i$ of AP $i$ by dividing the base capacity $C = 4.6\text{ Mbit/s}$ by $1 + \sum_{j \ne i, \text{ch}(j)=\text{ch}(i)} w(d_{ij})$, with piecewise-linear distance weights between $d_1 = 30\text{ m}$ and $d_2 = 60\text{ m}$. Because Mininet-WiFi does not natively produce co-channel contention in this setup, the testbed enforces the exact same $C_i$ formula as a downlink rate cap on each AP's traffic-control queue whenever a same-channel neighbour is up. While the text acknowledges that agreement between the twin and testbed validates only the implementation and queueing logic rather than physical contention, two structural consequences warrant attention in the system model:
   - First, because Eq. (1) depends only on AP administrative state and inter-AP distance $d_{ij}$ rather than on the interfering AP's traffic load $\lambda_f$ or utilization $U(t)$, a completely idle co-channel AP penalizes its neighbours identically to a saturated one (by up to $50\%$ when $d_{ij} \le 30\text{ m}$). This makes low- and medium-impact traffic management actions (client steering and rate limiting) mathematically incapable of relieving co-channel contention in both the twin and the testbed, ensuring by construction that only high-impact actions (channel change or AP shutdown) can alter $C_i$. Weighting $w(d_{ij})$ by the interfering AP's offered load or utilization in both the testbed shaper and Eq. (1) would make the contention model responsive to load-balancing actions, as in physical CSMA/CA networks.
   - Second, in the baseline scenario (Section III-A), `ap1` and `ap4` both operate on channel 1 at a separation of $d_{14} = 50\text{ m}$, which yields $w(50) = (60 - 50)/(60 - 30) = 1/3$ and reduces both APs' nominal capacity from $4.6\text{ Mbit/s}$ to $3.45\text{ Mbit/s}$ from $t = 0\text{ s}$ onward across all scenarios; making this baseline capacity reduction explicit in Section III-A or IV-A would clarify why `ap1` saturates early during the flash crowd.

6. **[I2.6] Missing Action-to-KPI Mappings, Edge-Case Singularities, and Unspecified Parameters Required for Reproducibility (Sections III-B, IV-A, IV-B, and V):**
   To make the analytical twin, heuristics, and intent pipeline self-contained and independently reproducible from the text alone without inspecting the codebase, several omitted mathematical definitions, edge-case guards, and model parameters should be explicitly stated:
   - *Missing Analytical Twin Mappings for `reroute` and `transmit power` Actions (Sections III-B & IV-A):* Section III-B defines seven allow-listed action types (`reroute`, `QoS queue`, `rate limit`, `client steering`, `transmit power`, `channel change`, and `AP admin state`), noting that `reroute` (low impact) is applied automatically when the twin accepts it and `transmit power` (medium impact) is simulated in the twin. However, Eqs. (1)–(4) model only wireless AP downlink capacities $C_i$, wireless priority queues $q$, and AP client counts $n_i$, without modeling wired OpenFlow switch links/paths or linking transmit power to $w(d_{ij})$ or $C_i$. If Eqs. (1)–(4) are invariant to `reroute` and `transmit power` (yielding $\hat{\mathbf{k}} = \mathbf{k}(s_t)$ and $\delta_k = 0$ for all $k$), Eq. (6) will trivially accept every such action via $\forall k : \delta_k \ge -5$. How $\hat{\mathbf{k}} = f(s, a)$ models these two action types should be specified.
   - *Singularity at $100\%$ Loss and Loss Clamping in Video Demand Reconstruction (Section IV-A, Eq. 2):* Section IV-A (*Sharing*) defines the demand $\lambda_f$ of a measured video flow as "its received rate divided by $1 - \ell_f$ (what its source must have sent)," and later (*Latency*) states that "A flow without a working AP is dead (no throughput, 100% loss)." When $\ell_f = 1$ ($100\%$ loss, or $0$ received rate), dividing the received rate by $1 - \ell_f$ yields $0/0$ (indeterminate), and if guarded to $0$, the twin would predict that steering a disconnected or completely starved video flow onto a working AP adds zero load. Additionally, the inelastic loss formula $\ell_f = 1 - x_f/\lambda_f + 0.002$ exceeds $1.0$ ($100.2\%$) when $x_f = 0$ unless explicitly clamped via $\min(1, 1 - x_f/\lambda_f + 0.002)$, and reconstructing $\lambda_f$ without accounting for the $0.2\%$ base loss introduces a slight mismatch in the no-action baseline $\mathbf{k}(s_t) = f(s_t, \emptyset)$. The fallback nominal source rate for video flows (when unmeasured or at $\ell_f = 1$) and the upper-bound clamp on $\ell_f$ should be stated.
   - *Unspecified Model, Topology, and Algorithm Parameters (Sections III–V):*
     1. *Spatial coordinates and log-distance path-loss parameters (Sections III-A, IV-A & IV-B):* Constraint (S1) enforces a $-75\text{ dBm}$ predicted signal threshold and Section V ranks stations by predicted signal using a log-distance path-loss model, but the 2D coordinates of the 4 APs and 20 stations (beyond `ap1` and `ap4` being $50\text{ m}$ apart), transmit power $P_{\text{tx}}$, reference distance/loss, and path-loss exponent $\gamma$ are omitted.
     2. *Web and video configured demands (Section IV-A):* Unlike bulk TCP ($0.394\text{ Mbit/s}$), the nominal configured demand $\lambda_f$ for video flows and the "configured fetch demand" for web flows in Eqs. (2)–(3), as well as the mapping of the 20 stations to traffic classes and zones and how Eq. (2) is evaluated if multiple web flows compete in the same queue, are not given.
     3. *Watch noise floors (Section IV-B, S4):* The calibrated numerical values of the per-KPI noise floors ("the 95th percentile of natural worsening in windows without an action") for $(T, L_{95}, \bar{\ell}, J)$ and the duration of the pre-action baseline window required alongside the $10\%$ threshold to trigger a rollback in (S4) are not reported.
     4. *Heuristic channel selection, policy compiler rules, and forecasting/detection hyperparameters (Sections IV-B & V):* For the channel-change heuristic, the candidate channel set (presumably $\{1, 6, 11\}$) and tie-breaking rule when multiple channels share the minimum distance-weighted co-channel load $\sum w(d_{ij})$ are not stated. Likewise, Section IV-B omits the verifier checks for operator policy constraints/objectives ("omitted here"), and Section V omits the deterministic compiler mapping from JSON `Policy` fields (`scope`, `objectives`, `hard constraints`) to the seven allow-listed action types, as well as the smoothing weights for Holt's linear smoothing, the moving-average window size, and the input telemetry features/hyperparameters for the $30\text{ s}$ Isolation Forest.
