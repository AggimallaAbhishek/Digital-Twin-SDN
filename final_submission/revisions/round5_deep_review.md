# HIGH LEVEL SUMMARY

## Summary
This paper presents a twin-gated closed control loop for software-defined campus Wi-Fi networks (SD-WLANs) that verifies candidate actions proposed by a heuristic optimizer or compiled from an LLM-translated operator intent inside an analytical digital twin before deployment, paired with a post-deployment KPI watch-and-rollback mechanism. Evaluated across 27 runs on a Mininet-WiFi and Ryu testbed spanning three disruption scenarios (lecture flash crowd, access-point failure, and co-channel interference), the work provides a transparent and reproducible empirical audit of what succeeded and what failed in closed-loop wireless automation. While the analytical twin achieves sub-millisecond evaluation times (median 0.61 ms) and 4.5% mean absolute percentage error on held-out steady-state per-flow throughput, its closed-loop predictions during the 30 s watch window prove no more accurate than a "nothing changes" baseline and fail to reject 21 of 23 harmful actions, leaving the watch-and-rollback mechanism as the primary—though imperfect—safety defense.

## Strengths
* **Rigorous and Reproducible Reporting of Negative Empirical Results:** The paper provides an exceptionally transparent, reproducible empirical audit of failure modes in closed-loop wireless automation—complete with public code, datasets, and build scripts—demonstrating why low-complexity steady-state models fail to serve as effective safety gates during live wireless transients and how naive rollback logic can fail when topology faults occur mid-watch.
* **Practical End-to-End Safety Architecture for Wireless SDN and Intent Compilation:** The integration of a fast (sub-millisecond) analytical airtime-and-queueing verifier with an append-only execution ledger, post-deployment watch-and-rollback monitoring, and a schema-constrained, deterministically compiled LLM intent interface offers a well-structured defense-in-depth blueprint that completely isolates the LLM from direct network execution.
* **Clear Identification of Actionable Control-Loop Vulnerabilities:** By analyzing every individual action across the 27 runs rather than relying solely on aggregate averages, the authors pinpoint concrete operational hazards—such as rolling steered stations back onto a newly failed access point and conflating exogenous disruptions with action-induced regressions—and formulate clear engineering fixes for future controllers.

## Weaknesses
* **[W1] Confounding of the Digital Twin Gate and Operator Approval Gate Between V2 and V3:** The experimental comparison between the "no twin" controller (V2) and the "full" controller (V3) modifies two variables simultaneously by adding both the digital twin verifier (S2) and a mandatory human-operator hold (S3) for high-impact channel changes in an unattended campaign; please consider adding an auto-approving V3 variant (or an operator-gated V2 variant) to cleanly isolate the standalone effect of the digital twin in the co-channel interference scenario.
* **[W2] Structural Blind Spots in the Steady-State Analytical Twin Formulation:** Because the analytical twin relies on a load-independent co-channel capacity cap enforced identically by the testbed, omits CSMA/CA contention and client re-association transients, and uses a calibrated M/M/1/K latency surrogate capped at 15.8 ms (well below the 50 ms recovery threshold), it cannot predict severe delay violations or short-horizon closed-loop transients; the authors might consider comparing against or incorporating a transient-aware or learned queueing surrogate (Reference Paper: Performance Analysis of the IEEE 802.11 Distributed Coordination Function, Giuseppe Bianchi, 2000; Reference Paper: RouteNet-Fermi: Network Modeling With Graph Neural Networks, Miquel Ferriol-Galmés et al., 2023; Reference Paper: Colosseum as a Digital Twin: Bridging Real-World Experimentation and Wireless Network Emulation, Davide Villa et al., 2024).
* **[W3] Incremental Architectural Novelty Relative to Prior Digital Twin and Verify-Then-Act Frameworks:** While adapting pre-deployment twin verification and schema-constrained LLM compilation to SD-WLAN client steering and airtime QoS is practical, the core paradigm builds closely on established Network Digital Twin architectures, centralized WLAN control loops, and verify-then-act LLM intent pipelines (Reference Paper: Network Digital Twin: Context, Enabling Technologies, and Opportunities, Paul Almasan et al., 2022; Reference Paper: Centralized Association and Channel Assignment in Software-Defined Wireless LANs: A Survey, Seyyedali Hosseini et al., 2018; Reference Paper: When Digital Twin Meets Generative AI: Intelligent Closed-Loop Network Management, Xinyu Huang et al., 2024; Reference Paper: Intent-Based Management of Next-Generation Networks: An LLM-Centric Approach, Abdelkader Mekrache et al., 2024; Reference Paper: NetInspector: Grounding LLM Reasoning in Verifiable Network State for Safe Intent-Based Networking, Yuxuan Wang et al., 2026).
* **[W4] Disconnect Between Offline LLM Schema Evaluation and End-to-End Twin Verification:** The LLM intent pipeline is evaluated only offline on a small 30-intent synthetic test set for JSON schema translation accuracy rather than inside the closed loop, and the 9 schema-valid semantic misses produced by the local fallback model (`qwen2.5:3b`) were never compiled and passed through the digital twin verifier to test whether the twin gate catches semantic translation errors.
* **[W5] Calibration Seed Overlap, Small Sample Size, and Missing Native Roaming Baseline:** The closed-loop campaign evaluates only three seeds per scenario that reuse the exact seed numbers (`42, 43, 44`) of the uncontrolled calibration dataset and compares against a plain SDN baseline (V1) without native IEEE 802.11k/v/r client roaming (forcing all V1 recovery times to be right-censored), so running fresh out-of-sample seeds and including a native roaming baseline would strengthen the statistical evaluation.

### [Analytical Digital Twin and Safe Control Problem Formulation (Section IV)] (Pages: 3-4)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **[I2.1] Inconsistent Priority-Queue Capacity Accounting, Elastic TCP Load, and Delay Scaling Between Equations (2) and (3)**
   In Section IV-A, comparing the strict-priority capacity sharing in Equation (2) against the M/M/1/K queueing delay surrogate in Equation (3) reveals three potential modeling inconsistencies:
   - **Double-counting dropped higher-priority traffic in $\rho_q$:** Under *Sharing*, the residual capacity available to priority queue $q$ subtracts the *granted* throughputs $x_f$ of higher-priority queues $q' \prec q$:
     $$R_q = \max\left(0, C_i - \sum_{q' \prec q} \sum_{f \in q'} x_f\right)$$
     Because each flow's allocation $x_f = \min(\lambda_f, \theta_q)$ is constrained by available capacity, $\sum_{q' \prec q} \sum_{f \in q'} x_f$ cannot exceed $C_i$. However, under *Latency*, the queueing load $\rho_q$ for queue $q$ sums the *uncapped offered demands* $\lambda_f$ across all higher- or equal-priority queues:
     $$\rho_q = \frac{\sum_{q' \preceq q} \sum_{f \in q'} \lambda_f}{C_i}$$
     In a finite-buffer system ($K = 6$) where a higher-priority queue $q' \prec q$ is oversubscribed ($\sum_{f \in q'} \lambda_f > C_i$), packets exceeding the buffer or rate limit ($\lambda_f - x_f$) are dropped at queue $q'$ rather than consuming link airtime ahead of lower-priority queues $q \succ q'$. Summing unthrottled $\lambda_f$ rather than served rates $x_f$ across $q' \prec q$ allows dropped higher-priority traffic to artificially inflate the saturation load $\rho_q$ and predicted delay $d_q$ of lower-priority queues.
   - **Fixed elastic TCP demand ceiling vs. open-loop Poisson load:** For an elastic bulk TCP flow, $\lambda_f$ is fixed at $0.394\text{ Mbit/s}$ ("the median measured on the training runs"), and the text notes that under congestion an elastic flow "slows down and sees only the base loss." Modeling elastic TCP with a fixed $\lambda_f = 0.394\text{ Mbit/s}$ creates a dual limitation: (i) in Equation (2), when client steering or a channel change frees capacity on an AP (up to $4.6\text{ Mbit/s}$), a greedy TCP flow's predicted throughput remains artificially capped at $0.394\text{ Mbit/s}$ (contributing to the $12\text{--}21\%$ bulk TCP error reported in Section VII-C); and (ii) in Equation (3), when an AP is congested ($x_f < 0.394\text{ Mbit/s}$), using the unthrottled $\lambda_f = 0.394\text{ Mbit/s}$ in $\rho_q$ treats a closed-loop, rate-adapting TCP flow as an open-loop Poisson arrival process, overstating $\rho_q$ and $d_q$. Replacing $\lambda_f$ with $x_f$ for higher-priority queues ($q' \prec q$) and rate-adapted elastic flows in $\rho_q$, while allowing greedy elastic demands in Equation (2) up to $C_i$ (or the active rate limit), would make Equations (2) and (3) internally consistent.
   - **Invariance of the delay scale factor $\sigma$ to co-channel capacity reductions:** In Equation (3), $d_q = d_0 + \sigma L(\rho_q, K)$ uses a constant scale factor $\sigma = 2.5\text{ ms}$ independent of the AP's effective capacity $C_i$ in Equation (1). By Little's Law, the service time required to drain $L(\rho_q, K)$ queued packets scales inversely with the link service rate $C_i$ ($\propto C / C_i$). Keeping $\sigma$ constant rather than scaling it by $C / C_i$ means that when co-channel interference reduces $C_i$, the saturation delay ceiling remains fixed at $d_0 + \sigma K = 15.8\text{ ms}$ rather than reflecting slower packet serialization and queue drain rates on the degraded link.

2. **[I2.2] Asymmetric Base-Loss Accounting, Unbounded Demand Reconstruction, and Uncapped Forward Loss in Section IV-A (Sharing)**
   In Section IV-A (*Sharing*), the inverse reconstruction of video demand $\lambda_f$ from live telemetry and the forward prediction of packet loss $\ell_f$ exhibit three mathematical edge cases that represent potential concerns:
   - **Unbounded demand amplification near $100\%$ loss:** The source demand $\lambda_f$ of a measured video flow is reconstructed as its received rate $x_{f,\text{meas}}$ divided by $1 - \ell_{f,\text{meas}}$, falling back to the class's configured rate ($1.0\text{ Mbit/s}$) only when the flow has "no throughput or with $100\%$ loss." During severe transient congestion where measured loss is close to—but strictly below—$100\%$ (e.g., $\ell_{f,\text{meas}} \in [0.96, 0.99]$) while residual received throughput $x_{f,\text{meas}} > 0$, dividing by $1 - \ell_{f,\text{meas}}$ multiplies $x_{f,\text{meas}}$ by $25\times\text{--}100\times$ without an explicit upper bound in the text. If byte counters and loss windows are slightly misaligned over the $10\text{ s}$ telemetry window, $\lambda_f$ can spike far above the physical source rate of $1.0\text{ Mbit/s}$ unless explicitly clamped at the configured class rate ($\lambda_f \le 1.0\text{ Mbit/s}$).
   - **Forward loss exceeding $100\%$ at zero allocation:** The forward loss prediction for an inelastic video flow is defined as $\ell_f = 1 - x_f / \lambda_f$ plus a base loss of $0.2\%$ ($0.002$). Without an explicit upper cap on the forward formula, whenever $x_f / \lambda_f < 0.002$ (including when a queue is starved of capacity so $R_q = 0$ and $x_f = 0$), the formula yields $\ell_f \in (1.0, 1.002]$ ($100.2\%$), exceeding $100\%$. Writing $\ell_f = \min(1, 1 - x_f / \lambda_f + 0.002)$ or formulating baseline link loss and congestion drop multiplicatively as $\ell_f = 1 - (1 - 0.002)(x_f / \lambda_f)$ would keep predicted loss within $[0, 1]$.
   - **Fixed-point shift in the simulated no-action baseline $f(s_t, \emptyset)$:** Because the inverse estimator $\lambda_f = x_{f,\text{meas}} / (1 - \ell_{f,\text{meas}})$ does not subtract the $0.2\%$ base loss (which the authors note is a "slight overestimate of $\lambda_f$"), feeding this reconstructed $\lambda_f$ into the twin's no-action baseline simulation $\mathbf{k}(s_t) = f(s_t, \emptyset)$ produces a systematic offset: on an uncongested AP ($R_q \ge \sum \lambda_f$), Equation (2) allocates $\hat{x}_f = \lambda_f = x_{f,\text{meas}} / (1 - \ell_{f,\text{meas}}) > x_{f,\text{meas}}$ (inflating baseline throughput above the live measurement), whereas on a capacity-constrained AP where $\hat{x}_f = x_{f,\text{meas}}$, the forward loss formula evaluates $1 - \hat{x}_f / \lambda_f = \ell_{f,\text{meas}}$ and then adds the $0.2\%$ base loss a second time ($\hat{\ell}_f = \ell_{f,\text{meas}} + 0.002$). Subtracting the base loss during reconstruction—e.g., $\lambda_f = x_{f,\text{meas}} / (1 - \max(0, \ell_{f,\text{meas}} - 0.002))$ or using the multiplicative inverse—would make the inverse demand estimator exact with respect to the forward model.

3. **[I2.3] Ambiguity and KPI Weighting Distortion in Web Flow Throughput Reporting (Section IV-A, Sharing)**
   Section IV-A states that a web flow participates in the max-min fair sharing of Equation (2) and in $\rho_q$ with its configured fetch demand ($\lambda_f = 0.08\text{ Mbit/s}$), "but the rate it reports is that of one page fetch, $0.8$ of the capacity that the other traffic in its own or a higher queue leaves free." This formulation raises two potential concerns for reproducibility and KPI evaluation:
   - **Algebraic ambiguity under intra-queue and cross-queue contention:** It is unclear from the prose whether "the capacity that the other traffic in its own or a higher queue leaves free" subtracts the allocated rates of all flows except the single web flow $f$ being evaluated (i.e., $0.8 \times \max(0, R_q - \sum_{f' \in q \setminus \{f\}} x_{f'})$) or excludes all concurrent web flows in queue $q$, whether multiple concurrent web flows on the same AP each report $0.8$ of that full residual capacity without dividing it among themselves, and whether lower-priority queues $q'' \succ q$ have their residual capacity $R_{q''}$ reduced by the web flow's steady allocation $x_f \le 0.08\text{ Mbit/s}$ or by its reported burst fetch rate.
   - **Distortion of mean throughput $T$ in the KPI vector $\mathbf{k}$:** On a lightly loaded AP ($C_i = 4.6\text{ Mbit/s}$), $0.8$ of the free capacity is up to $3.68\text{ Mbit/s}$ per web flow—$46\times$ its configured demand $\lambda_f = 0.08\text{ Mbit/s}$. Because the first component $T$ of the KPI vector $\mathbf{k} = (T, L_{95}, \bar{\ell}, J)$ is an unweighted arithmetic mean of per-flow throughputs across the action's scope, averaging a web flow's instantaneous page-fetch burst rate alongside steady-state video ($1.0\text{ Mbit/s}$) and bulk TCP ($0.394\text{ Mbit/s}$) flow rates causes $T$ (and its relative change $\delta_T$ in Equation 6) to be heavily dominated by residual link capacity on stations with web flows rather than actual delivered traffic throughput. Providing the explicit algebraic formula for a web flow's reported rate and clarifying how it enters $T$ would remove this ambiguity.

4. **[I2.4] Structural Mismatches Between the Pre-Deployment Acceptance Rule (S2, Eq. 6), the Post-Deployment Rollback Rule (S4), and the Control Objective (Eq. 5)**
   Although the authors note that Equation (6) is deliberately permissive and test stricter rules in Section VII-B, the formal specification of the safe control problem in Section IV-B exhibits three structural inconsistencies that warrant discussion:
   - **Disjunctive acceptance (S2) vs. conjunctive rollback (S4) and $\pm\infty$ boundary conflicts:** The pre-deployment verifier predicate in Equation (6),
     $$\text{accept} \iff (\forall k : \delta_k \ge -5) \lor (\exists k : \delta_k > 5),$$
     is disjunctive, whereas the post-deployment watch condition (S4) is conjunctive (rolling back an action if *any* single KPI worsens by $>10\%$ and exceeds its noise floor). Because client-count fairness $J$ (Equation 4) is included in the existential clause $(\exists k : \delta_k > 5)$ and depends solely on station association counts $n_i$ (ignoring channel capacity $C_i$, interference, and flow demands), any client-steering action that improves $J$ by $>5\%$ satisfies Equation (6) even if the twin simultaneously predicts severe regressions ($\delta_k < -10\%$ or $-\infty$) in throughput, latency, or loss that (S4) is designed to roll back. Similarly, below Equation (6), $\delta_k$ is set to $+\infty$ or $-\infty$ when $k = 0 \ne \hat{k}$; under the logical OR in Equation (6), any transition from $k = 0$ to an arbitrarily small improvement $\hat{k} > 0$ yields $\delta_k = +\infty > 5$, forcing acceptance even if another metric simultaneously hits $\delta_{k'} = -\infty$.
   - **Absence of a noise floor in Equation (6) near the $0.2\%$ base loss:** For latency and loss, Equation (6) evaluates purely relative percentage changes $\delta_k = 100(k - \hat{k})/|k|$ without a noise floor. Because every active flow in the analytical twin has at least the $0.2\%$ base loss ($k = \bar{\ell} \ge 0.20\%$), the $k = 0$ branch is bypassed whenever active flows exist, and a negligible $0.02\text{ percentage-point}$ increase in simulated loss on an uncongested AP (from $0.20\%$ to $0.22\%$) registers as a $-10\%$ relative regression ($\delta_{\bar{\ell}} = -10 < -5$). This violates the first clause of Equation (6) even though a $0.02\text{ percentage-point}$ change is $36\times$ smaller than the $0.72\text{ percentage-point}$ natural noise floor enforced for loss in (S4). Conversely, a tiny drop from $0.22\%$ to $0.20\%$ yields $\delta_{\bar{\ell}} = +9.1\% > 5\%$, satisfying the second clause of Equation (6) by itself. Incorporating the (S4) noise floors directly into Equation (6) would align the pre- and post-deployment predicates.
   - **Disconnect from the recovery objective ($T_{\text{rec}}$, Equation 5):** The control problem in Section IV-B seeks to minimize $T_{\text{rec}}$ in Equation (5), which requires bringing the affected AP's utilization $\mathcal{U}(\tau) < 0.8$ and $L_{95}(\tau) \le 50\text{ ms}$. Because the twin's latency prediction $d_q$ in Equation (3) is capped at $d_0 + \sigma K = 15.8\text{ ms} < 50\text{ ms}$, $\mathcal{U}(\tau) < 0.8$ is the sole recovery threshold in Equation (5) that the twin is capable of crossing, yet AP utilization $\mathcal{U}$ is omitted from the verifier's KPI vector $\mathbf{k} = (T, L_{95}, \bar{\ell}, J)$ in Equation (6). Furthermore, the twin's $L_{95}$ metric is computed over stations using each station's *highest-priority* queue rather than over video flows—meaning a QoS action that promotes a bulk or web flow on a station changes that station's reported video latency $L_{95}$ in the twin.

### [Control Loop Methodology, LLM Pipeline, and Experimental Setup (Sections V–VI)] (Pages: 4-6)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **[I3.1] Algorithmic Vulnerabilities in Watch-and-Rollback Preconditions, Delayed Approvals, Watch-Window Locking, and Multi-Action Ticks (Section IV-B and Section V, Algorithm 1 and "Safety enforcement"):**
   - *Potential Concern:* Several aspects of the formal safety preconditions in Section IV-B (S1, S3, S4) and the closed-loop execution and monitoring logic in Section V (Algorithm 1 and "Safety enforcement") introduce structural control-loop vulnerabilities during overlapping network disruptions or multi-proposal ticks:
     1. *Exemption of Rollbacks (S4) and Delayed Approvals (S3) from Hard Safety Invariant (S1) and Unchecked Topological Validity During Disruptions:* Constraint (S1) defines the state-dependent admissible action set $a_t \in \mathcal{A}(s_t)$ (including client-share bounds, source-AP association, predicted signal $\ge -75\text{ dBm}$, transmit-power steps, and keeping at least one AP per zone), though its prose definition does not explicitly state the precondition that a target AP of a steer must be operationally *up* ($\text{up}_{\text{target}} = 1$). However, (S3) applies operator-approved actions at a later tick $t + \Delta t$ without re-checking $a_t \in \mathcal{A}(s_{t+\Delta t})$, and in Algorithm 1 (line 1: `finish due watches: if a KPI regressed (S4) roll back, else keep`), when an action's 30 s watch window detects a KPI regression exceeding 10% and the noise floor, the executor automatically executes inverse rollback actions at $t + 30\text{ s}$ "without checking (S1) again, so the rollback assumes the topology did not change during the watch" or verifying whether the rollback target AP is still up. In addition, lines 2–4 of Algorithm 1 (`if an earlier change is still being watched then return`) suspend new proposals for the duration of the 30 s watch without checking whether an external infrastructure fault occurred during that window. When an AP fails during the 30 s watch window of an earlier client-steering action, the watch misattributes the fault's KPI degradation to the steer, rolls the steered stations back onto the now-down source AP (leaving them disconnected for the rest of the run), and blocks the loop from reacting to the actual fault until the watch expires.
     2. *Sequential Single-Proposal Verification and Unattributed Watch Windows on Multi-Action Ticks:* In Algorithm 1 (lines 7–14) and the "Safety enforcement" subsection, up to 3 heuristic proposals $p \in P$ can be verified and applied within a single 5 s tick. However, each proposal is verified individually at line 8 against the same unmodified pre-tick state snapshot $s$ (`simulate` $\hat{\mathbf{k}} = f(s, p)$) rather than sequentially updating the simulated state after each accepted proposal in the same tick (`s <- simulate_state(s, p)`), ignoring inter-action coupling on shared channels, target AP load, or network-wide fairness $J$ (unlike LLM-compiled policies, which are verified jointly). Moreover, when multiple actions are applied in the same tick, each starts its own 30 s watch over the same overlapping time window, making it impossible for the executor to isolate which concurrent proposal caused an observed regression.
   - *Impact & Suggested Improvement:* Even though the empirical consequences of these behaviors are transparently documented as negative results in Sections VII–VIII, the formulation in Section IV-B and methodology in Section V would be strengthened by formalizing the safety fixes directly in (S1)–(S4) and Algorithm 1: (a) formally defining $\text{up}_{\text{target}} = 1$ in $\mathcal{A}(s_t)$, enforcing $a_\tau \in \mathcal{A}(s_\tau)$ on all state transitions executed at tick $\tau$ (whether forward, delayed under S3, or rolled back under S4 at line 1 of Algorithm 1), and immediately cancelling any active watch and releasing the loop lock if a verifiable infrastructure event (such as an AP transitioning from `up` to `down` after 5 s of silence) occurs within the action's scope during $[t, t + 30\text{ s}]$; and (b) either updating the simulated state $s$ sequentially across proposals within a tick (or verifying them jointly as $f(s, P_{\text{accepted}})$) or restricting the heuristic loop to at most 1 applied action per watch cycle.

2. **[I3.2] Reuse of Random Seeds Across Calibration and Closed-Loop Evaluation Datasets (Section VI, "Scenarios and seeds", and Section VII-A):**
   - *Potential Concern:* Section VI ("Scenarios and seeds", page 5) states that the three closed-loop evaluation scenarios are run with random seeds 42, 43, and 44 under each controller variant, and immediately notes that the digital twin, Holt forecaster, and Isolation Forest anomaly detector were calibrated/trained on a separate dataset of 12 recorded runs without control using the exact same seeds (42, 43, and 44 as the training, validation, and test split, alongside fixed station-position seed 7). Because the campus scenario scripts, walker mobility paths, and traffic generation are seeded deterministically by these numbers, the evaluation runs share identical initial station placements, mobility trajectories, and traffic arrival processes with the calibration dataset prior to the first controller action (including the identical first-minute startup steers in Fig. 11 before $t_d$)—and throughout the entire 600 s run for baseline V1 ("plain SDN"), which takes no control actions.
   - *Impact & Suggested Improvement:* Evaluating closed-loop controllers on the same pseudo-random seed sequences used to calibrate the twin's parameters ($\sigma$, bulk TCP demand) and the watch step's 95th-percentile natural worsening noise floors (in S4) introduces potential calibration overlap (with seed `44` being the only strictly out-of-sample seed relative to the training/validation calibration splits `42` and `43`), and using only three seeds per scenario precludes statistical confidence intervals in the presence of right-censored and bimodal outcomes. Explicitly noting in Section VII-A that seed `44` is the only out-of-sample seed relative to the training/validation splits, and running a supplementary check or evaluation campaign on an independent set of fresh random seeds (e.g., seeds `45, 46, 47` or 10–20 unseen seeds strictly disjoint from calibration seeds 42, 43, and 44) would eliminate overlap on the baseline trajectories and strengthen statistical power.

3. **[I3.3] Omission of a Native Client-Side Roaming (IEEE 802.11k/v/r) Baseline and Resulting Right-Censoring of Recovery Times (Section VI, Table I and "Metrics"):**
   - *Potential Concern:* In Table I, the baseline controller V1 ("plain SDN") relies solely on L2 learning, where stations re-associate only when their AP goes completely down and never roam away from an overloaded or co-channel-interfered AP on their own. Consequently, stations in V1 remain permanently attached to saturated APs in the lecture flash-crowd and co-channel interference scenarios, guaranteeing that V1 never recovers within the 600 s horizon and forcing all nine V1 recovery times ($T_{\text{rec}}$) to be right-censored at the end of the run ($T_{\text{end}} - t_d = 390, 360,\text{ or } 420\text{ s}$).
   - *Impact & Suggested Improvement:* While this scope boundary is acknowledged in the text, omitting native client-side roaming or IEEE 802.11k/v/r BSS transition management leaves open how the SDN control loop compares against standard distributed Wi-Fi roaming, and compresses the measurable improvement window for testing Hypothesis 1 (which requires V3's mean recovery time to be at least 30% below V1's capped time). Including a baseline variant in Table I where stations use standard threshold-based client roaming or native 802.11k/v/r steering when AP utilization or packet loss exceeds a threshold would provide an operational baseline capable of recovering within the 600 s window.

4. **[I3.4] Underspecified Details in the Heuristic Proposal Rules, Deterministic Policy Compiler, and Intent Dataset (Sections V and VI):**

     1. *Client-Steering Budget Partitioning and Proposal Ordering in `PROPOSE(s)` (Section V):* Section V states that an AP at or above 0.8 utilization sheds up to 30% of its clients (rounded down) "as one proposal per target AP; the targets are the APs below 0.6, least utilized first, and the stations moved are those with the strongest predicted signal at the target; the budget is shared by the targets, so no station is proposed twice."    - *Impact & Suggested Improvement:* Briefly stating the multi-target budget allocation, proposal ordering, and operator-queue deduplication rules in Section V, summarizing the deterministic compiler's mapping logic in Section V, and documenting the prompt structure and construction of `intents.jsonl` in Section VI would make the methodology self-contained.

#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **[I4.1] Confounded Controller Comparison Between `V2` and `V3` Across Verification (S2) and Operator Gates (S3) (Abstract, Sections I, III-B, V, VI Table I, VII-A, VII-B, VIII, Table III, and Figs. 11–13)**
   - **Verification Summary:** The action ledger counts across Table III, Table IV, and Figs. 11–14 are mathematically consistent: `V2` proposes $64$ actions ($38$ kept, $22$ rolled back, $1$ failed rollback, $3$ still watched = $61$ resolved) and `V3` proposes $65$ actions ($33$ kept, $18$ rolled back, $2$ failed rollbacks, $4$ rejected and not applied, $8$ awaiting an operator = $53$ resolved), summing to $114$ resolved actions ($71$ kept + $40$ rolled back + $3$ failed rollbacks) and $129$ total twin verdicts ($64 + 65$). Likewise, the relative recovery-time reductions of `V3` over `V1` ($1 - 359/390 = 7.95\% \approx 8\%$, $1 - 300/360 = 16.67\% \approx 17\%$, and $0\%$) and the one-sided Fisher exact test on `V2`'s $61$ resolved actions ($2/3$ harmful among twin-rejected vs. $21/58$ harmful among twin-accepted, $p = \frac{\binom{23}{2}\binom{38}{1} + \binom{23}{3}\binom{38}{0}}{\binom{61}{3}} = \frac{11385}{35990} \approx 0.316 \approx 0.32$) are exact.
   - **Potential Concern:** In formulating and evaluating Hypotheses 1 and 2 (Abstract, Section I, Section III-B, Table I, and Sections VII-A and VII-B), controller variants `V2 no twin` ("introduced to measure what the twin prevents") and `V3 full` differ simultaneously across two independent mechanisms: `V3` enforces the digital twin's verification verdict (rule S2) *and* holds high-impact actions (channel changes and taking an AP down) behind a mandatory human-operator approval gate (rule S3), whereas `V2` bypasses both gates and applies all heuristic proposals directly. While Table I and Sections VII-A and VIII candidly note that `V2` versus `V3` in the co-channel interference scenario compares a loop with an absent operator against one without, pairing an unattended execution protocol with a mandatory operator hold in `V3` creates two broader analytical issues:
     1. *Structural Unattainability of H1 and Untested Twin Effect on High-Impact Recovery:* Under the capacity model in Eq. (1), client steering cannot restore capacity lost to co-channel interference ($C_i$ depends only on whether a same-channel AP is up, not on its traffic load). Consequently, when run unattended, `V3` is architecturally prevented from executing the only action capable of resolving the interference scenario ($\ge 420\text{ s}$ recovery and $25.7\%$ loss vs. $146\text{ s}$ recovery and $9.1\%$ loss for `V2`), making H1 unattainable by construction in the interference scenario regardless of the digital twin's accuracy and leaving the standalone effect of the digital twin on high-impact actions empirically untested.

Thus, `V2` and `V3` operate on substantially different post-disruption network states and action mixtures (`V2`'s $61$ resolved actions include $9$ applied channel changes; `V3`'s $53$ resolved actions consist solely of client steers). Pooling `V2` and `V3` into a single 114-action sample in Section VII-B and Figs. 12–13 mixes the twin's predictive accuracy with state divergence caused by the operator gate.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Explicitly distinguish the effect of the digital twin verifier from the operator gate upfront in Sections I and III-B, and because re-running a single variant's 9-run campaign takes roughly two hours via `make campaign`, consider adding an unconfounded ablation variant to Table I—either an auto-approving `V3` (`V3-auto`, which enforces the twin's verdict S2 but automatically approves S3 without delay) or an operator-gated `V2` (`V2-gated`, which holds high-impact actions for an operator while ignoring S2)—and reporting the closed-loop error distributions in Fig. 13 separately for `V2` and `V3` to cleanly isolate the digital twin gate from operator unavailability.

   - **Potential Concern:** Across the formal recovery definition $T_{\text{rec}}$ in Section IV-B (Eq. 5), the evaluation cohort definitions in Section VI ("Metrics", page 5), and the AP-failure results in Section VII-A (Table II and Fig. 4), plain SDN (`V1`) achieves the lowest packet loss ($4.8\%$ vs. $10.0\%$ for `V2` and $18.8\%$ for `V3`), the lowest 95th-percentile video latency ($18\text{ ms}$ vs. $348\text{ ms}$ for `V2` and $672\text{ ms}$ for `V3`), and the highest Jain's fairness index ($0.96$ vs. $0.91$ and $0.89$), yet `V1` is classified as never recovering in any run ($T_{\text{rec}} \ge 360\text{ s}$, $0/3$ runs recovered) while `V2` ($\ge 142\text{ s}$, $2/3$ recovered) and `V3` ($\ge 300\text{ s}$, $1/3$ recovered) are credited with faster recovery. Three methodological factors drive this outcome and warrant sharper specification across Sections IV-B, VI, and VII-A:
     1. *Unaligned Cohort Scopes and Load Shedding via Failed Rollbacks:* Section IV-B defines the affected AP for $\mathcal{U}(\tau) < 0.8$ in Eq. (5) as the one with the highest mean utilization in the $30\text{ s}$ after $t_d$ ("for the AP failure, necessarily a surviving one") without specifying in Eq. (5) or Section VI which exact set of flows $L_{95}(\tau) \le 50\text{ ms}$ is evaluated over inside Eq. (5), while Section VI fixes the Table II user-facing flow KPIs to the *affected flows cohort* (the stations originally associated with the *failed* `ap2` in the 30 s before $t_d$). When `ap2` fails at $t_d = 240\text{ s}$, total campus capacity drops by $25\%$ while demand stays constant, pushing the surviving AP's utilization just above $0.8$ under `V1` even though user-facing QoS ($L_{95} = 18\text{ ms} \le 50\text{ ms}$, loss $4.8\%$) remains well within service targets.      3. *Pre-Disruption Cohort Consistency Across Variants in the Flash Crowd:* In the lecture flash-crowd scenario, $t_d = 210\text{ s}$ (when video starts) while the ten walking students arrive between $120\text{ s}$ and $210\text{ s}$; defining the cohort dynamically from stations associated with `ap1` during the 30 s before $t_d$ could yield different station sets across V1, V2, and V3 if any steering occurs before $t_d$, unless the cohort is fixed deterministically per seed from the uncontrolled baseline.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Explicitly index the AP and flow cohort scopes in Eq. (5) (specifying time-varying capacity $C_i(\tau)$, which exact flow set defines $L_{95}(\tau)$ in Eq. 5, the latency assigned to a dead flow in Section IV, and whether the "affected flows" cohort per seed is extracted deterministically from V1 in Section VI). (5), separate categorical disconnection/timeout counts from continuous queueing latency in Table II and Fig. 4 (or flag the $348\text{ ms}$ and $672\text{ ms}$ means analogously to the $\ge$ censoring symbol), and consider reporting how recovery times behave under user-facing SLA criteria ($L_{95} \le 50\text{ ms}$ and packet loss) when hardware capacity is lost.

3. **[I4.3] Unvalidated Sub-Models, Analytical Blind Spots, and Fault-Tainted Counterfactuals in the Closed-Loop Audit (Sections VII-B and VII-C, Table IV, and Figs. 11–14)**
   - **Potential Concern:** Section VII-B shows that the twin's closed-loop predictions have higher median relative errors than a `"nothing changes"` baseline for throughput ($56.7\%$ vs. $46.5\%$), latency ($70.5\%$ vs. $62.3\%$), and loss ($57.2\%$ vs. $50.3\%$), and Table IV shows that stricter acceptance rules fail because only $11$ of `V2`'s $23$ harmful actions had any predicted regression at all.      2. *Latency Capping, Client-Count Fairness, and SNR-Insensitive Capacity:* Because the M/M/1/K latency surrogate in Eq.    - **Suggested Improvement:** Using the existing recorded datasets (`make results`), report the held-out offline validation errors for latency and packet loss alongside throughput in Section VII-C, clarify that the $4.0\%$ and $3.3\%$ figures in the text refer specifically to video flows (and explain why web flows are omitted from Fig. 14), and add a breakdown in Table IV restricted to `V2`'s $15$ untainted harmful actions and $31$ untainted kept actions (as well as testing a rule that excludes client-count fairness $J$ from the $\exists k : \delta_k > 5$ trigger). Expanding Section VII-B to connect the $15.8\text{ ms}$ delay cap, SNR-insensitive capacity sharing, and $10\text{ s}$ startup-burst telemetry windows to the false-positive twin verdicts—and checking whether comparing twin predictions against a later post-transient window ($30\text{–}60\text{ s}$ after kept actions) recovers steady-state accuracy—would substantially strengthen the negative-results analysis.
