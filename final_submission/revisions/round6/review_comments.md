# HIGH LEVEL SUMMARY
---
## Summary
This paper presents and empirically evaluates a safety-gated closed control loop for software-defined campus Wi-Fi networks (built on Mininet-WiFi and Ryu) in which candidate configuration changes—proposed either by a heuristic load-balancing optimizer or compiled from natural-language operator intents via an LLM—must pass an analytical digital twin verifier before deployment, followed by a 30-second live KPI watch-and-rollback mechanism. The analytical digital twin models distance-weighted co-channel capacity sharing, strict-priority max-min fair airtime allocation, and a calibrated finite-buffer M/M/1/K queueing delay surrogate, operating at a median inference time of 0.61 ms within a 5-second control loop. Evaluated across 27 emulation runs spanning three disruption scenarios (lecture flash crowd, access-point failure, and co-channel interference), the study transparently reports a set of negative empirical results: although the twin achieves a 4.5% mean absolute percentage error on steady-state held-out per-flow throughput, its closed-loop predictions over the 30-second watch window yield higher median errors than a naive "nothing changes" baseline (56.7% vs. 46.5% for throughput), reject only 2 of 23 harmful actions in the un-gated controller variant (failing Hypothesis H2), and do not achieve the targeted 30% recovery-time reduction (failing Hypothesis H1), leaving actual runtime protection largely to the reactive watch-and-rollback executor, which itself suffered 3 failed rollbacks onto a downed access point.

## Strengths
* **Transparent and Rigorous Reporting of Negative Results:** The authors provide an exceptionally candid and well-documented empirical analysis of where and why the minimal steady-state digital twin and reactive rollback mechanisms failed in closed-loop operation, offering valuable practical insights into the gap between offline steady-state accuracy and dynamic closed-loop safety gating.
* **Sub-Millisecond Analytical Twin and End-to-End Reproducibility:** The analytical formulation executes in a median of 0.61 ms without requiring GPU training pipelines, and the authors provide a complete, self-contained open-source artifact (`make campaign`, `make results`, `make paper`) with recorded execution ledgers that make every table and figure fully reproducible.
* **Defense-in-Depth Architecture for LLM Intent Translation:** By restricting the LLM strictly to translating natural-language intents into a JSON-schema-validated policy structure that is deterministically compiled into bounded actions and verified before execution, the system eliminates syntax and parameter-bound hallucinations while preventing the model from directly actuating the network.

## Weaknesses
* **[W1] Confounding of the Digital Twin Verifier and the Unattended Operator Gate Across Controller Variants:** In the experimental comparison between V2 ("no twin") and V3 ("full system"), V3 simultaneously introduces the digital twin KPI verifier (S2) and a mandatory human-operator approval gate for high-impact actions (S3) while running completely unattended. Consequently, in the co-channel interference scenario—where only a high-impact channel change can restore capacity—V2 applies channel changes directly and recovers in every run, whereas V3 holds all 8 twin-accepted channel changes indefinitely waiting for an absent operator, confounding the causal effect of the digital twin with the absent operator.
* **[W2] Over-Simplified Analytical Twin Physics and Capped Latency Surrogate:** The analytical capacity model enforces the exact same load-independent distance-weighted rate cap as the testbed's traffic-control queues while omitting MAC-layer contention and multi-rate anomalies, and the M/M/1/K delay surrogate is algebraically bounded at $15.8\text{ ms}$ (well below the $50\text{ ms}$ recovery threshold) and ignores client re-association transients. Please consider comparing or extending the twin against contention-aware 802.11 analytical models or learned queueing network surrogates to evaluate whether higher-fidelity models can effectively filter harmful actions in closed-loop control (Reference Paper: Performance Analysis of the IEEE 802.11 Distributed Coordination Function, Giuseppe Bianchi, 2000; Reference Paper: Performance Anomaly of 802.11b, M. Heusse et al., 2003; Reference Paper: RouteNet-Fermi: Network Modeling With Graph Neural Networks, Miquel Ferriol-Galmés et al., 2023; Reference Paper: DeepQueueNet: Towards Scalable and Generalized Network Performance Estimation with Packet-Level Visibility, Qingqing Yang et al., 2022).
* **[W3] Logical Vulnerability in the Disjunctive Verifier Rule and Unchecked Rollback Preconditions:** Because Jain's fairness index $J$ is computed exclusively over operational access points (Eq. 4) and the verifier acceptance rule (Eq. 6) uses a logical disjunction ($\lor$) without checking target-AP liveness as a hard bound in (S1), shedding clients onto a dead AP can increase $J$ across the surviving APs by $>5\%$ and be accepted by the verifier; similarly, executing watch-and-rollback reversals (S4) without re-verifying target-AP liveness caused the executor to blindly roll steered clients back onto a failed AP in all 3 failed rollbacks.
* **[W4] Calibration Seed Reuse and Absence of a Native Client-Roaming Baseline:** The 27-run evaluation campaign reuses the same three random seeds (42, 43, and 44) used to calibrate the twin parameters, noise floors, and anomaly detector (leaving only seed 44 strictly held out), and the baseline controller (V1) disables client-side roaming away from overloaded or interfered APs so that V1 never recovers within 600 s. Evaluating on fresh random seeds and including a baseline with standard network-assisted client roaming would avoid right-censored recovery times and strengthen statistical validity (Reference Paper: IEEE Standard for Information Technology—Telecommunications and Information Exchange Between Systems—Local and Metropolitan Area Networks—Specific Requirements—Part 11: Wireless LAN Medium Access Control (MAC) and Physical Layer (PHY) Specifications, IEEE LAN/MAN Standards Committee, 2021).
* **[W5] Offline Evaluation and In-Sample Prompt Tuning in the LLM Pipeline:** While the neuro-symbolic intent pipeline adapts established formal specification translation and verification patterns to wireless policies, the system prompt was revised after an initial pass on the same 30-intent evaluation set, none of the compiled QoS or rate-limit actions was applied in the closed loop, and the 9 schema-valid semantic misses from the local model (`qwen2.5:3b`) were not tested against the twin verifier; evaluating on a strictly held-out or public benchmark would better establish generalization (Reference Paper: NetConfEval: Can LLMs Facilitate Network Configuration?, Changjie Wang et al., 2024; Reference Paper: Intent-Based Management of Next-Generation Networks: An LLM-Centric Approach, Abdelkader Mekrache et al., 2024; Reference Paper: What do LLMs need to Synthesize Correct Router Configurations?, Rajdeep Mondal et al., 2023).

## Potential Issues And Suggestions
### [Framing, Related Work, Problem Scenario, and Synthesis of Limitations] (Pages: 1-3, 11-13)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **[I1.1] State Drift in Stateless Telemetry Reconstruction When QoS or Rate-Limit Actions Are Active (Section III-B, Section V, and Section VIII):**
   - **Potential Concern:** Section III-B (page 2) states that the digital twin rebuilds its state $s$ from the last $10\text{ s}$ of telemetry at every $5\text{ s}$ refresh so that it holds no accumulated state and *"cannot drift from the network."* In the same paragraph, however, the text notes: *"A flow's queue and rate limit are not read back: the rebuilt state puts every flow in best effort without a limit, which is exact for this campaign, where no QoS or rate-limit action was applied; they enter the state only when an action is simulated."* While this simplification is exact for the closed-loop heuristic campaign (which proposes only client steering and channel changes), Section III-B and Section V ("LLM roles," page 5) specify that an LLM policy compiles *exclusively* to `QoS queue` moves and `rate limit` actions (applied via the API as noted in Algorithm 1's caption) and retains other objectives/constraints on the policy object *"which the verifier checks at every loop."* Consequently, if an operator applies an LLM-compiled policy to the network as depicted in Fig. 2, the very next $5\text{ s}$ stateless telemetry rebuild on line 5 of Algorithm 1 will overwrite those applied QoS and rate-limit configurations by resetting all flows in $s$ back to best-effort without rate limits, causing the twin's representation (and its simulated no-action baseline $f(s_t, \emptyset)$) to immediately drift from the live network configuration.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Read back active OpenFlow/traffic-control queue assignments and rate limits during the $10\text{ s}$ telemetry state reconstruction (or merge the executor's ledger of active policies into the rebuilt state $s_t$ on line 5 of Algorithm 1), and note this state-reconstruction requirement in Section VIII alongside the other engineering fixes needed for end-to-end integration.

2. **[I1.2] Action-Space Disjointness and Unverified LLM-Compiled Actions Relative to the Architectural Framing (Title, Abstract, Section I, Section III-B, Fig. 2, Section VII-C, and Section VIII):**
   - **Potential Concern:** The paper's title (*"Verifying SDN and LLM-Proposed Actions in a Digital Twin Before They Reach the Network"*) and architecture diagram (Fig. 2) present a unified verification pipeline receiving proposals from both the LLM compiler and the heuristic optimizer. While the Abstract and Section I transparently state that the LLM was evaluated offline and none of its actions was applied to the network, two structural disconnects remain between the LLM pipeline, the action space, and the verifier:
     1. *Disjoint and unmodeled action types:* Section III-B defines seven allow-listed action types (`reroute`, `QoS queue`, `rate limit`, `client steering`, `transmit power`, `channel change`, `AP admin state`). However, the heuristic optimizer proposes only `client steering` and `channel change`, while the LLM compiler emits only `QoS queue` and `rate limit` actions. Moreover, as detailed in Section IV-A, `reroute` is unconditionally rejected because the campus topology has a single path between any two nodes, `transmit power` is accepted as a no-op because Eqs. (1)–(4) do not depend on it, and `rate limit` and `QoS queue` actions rely on unvalidated rate-limit, latency, and loss predictions (with the latency surrogate capped at $15.8\text{ ms}$, below the $50\text{ ms}$ recovery threshold).
     2. *Unverified compiled actions on the 30-intent benchmark:* **H3** evaluates only whether the LLM translates text into the intermediate JSON `Policy` schema; the actions compiled from the 30 test intents (including the 9 schema-valid semantic misses produced by the local fallback model `qwen2.5:3b`, Section VII-C and Section VIII) were never passed through the digital twin verifier to test whether the twin's KPI rule accepts or rejects them. Given that the deterministic compiler and analytical twin execute offline in a median of $0.61\text{ ms}$, compiling those 9 schema-valid semantic misses and evaluating them in the verifier against recorded test-split states would require no additional emulation time and would directly test whether the twin gate catches any semantic translation errors.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** In Section I and Section III-B (and the caption of Fig. 2), explicitly clarify which subset of the seven allow-listed action types is actively modeled and validated in the twin, and disclose in Section I that the 30-intent set was used during prompt iteration. In addition, passing the compiled actions from all 30 intents (including the 9 semantic misses from `qwen2.5:3b`) through the deterministic compiler and digital twin verifier offline is a lightweight experiment that would directly test how the verifier handles LLM-compiled policies.

3. **[I1.3] Scope of Watch-Cancellation Remedies in Section VIII (Section VIII, Page 12):**
   - **Potential Concern:** In Section VIII (page 12), the authors propose four fixes for the observed closed-loop failures, stating that *"a watch should not judge an action while an AP goes silent or down"* and that *"only verifiable infrastructure events should cancel it."* However, in the co-channel interference scenario, `ap3` switching to channel 1 at $t_d = 180\text{ s}$ is also a verifiable infrastructure event directly observable in AP telemetry (Section III-B), and across all scenarios, exogenous shifts in offered station demand $\lambda_f$ (e.g., video streams starting at $10\text{ s}$ or $210\text{ s}$) occurred during the $30\text{ s}$ watch window, causing valid steering actions to be rolled back.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Expand the discussion of watch-cancellation triggers in Section VIII to include all telemetry-verifiable infrastructure changes (such as external channel changes) as well as adjustments for measurable changes in offered source demand $\lambda_f$.

### [Mathematical Formulation of the Analytical Twin and Safe Control Problem] (Pages: 3-5)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **[I2.1] Logical Interaction Between Target-AP Liveness in `(S1)`, Fairness $J$ (Eq. 4), and the Disjunctive Acceptance Rule (Eq. 6)**
   - **Potential Concern:** In Subsection IV-B (page 4, right column), hard constraint `(S1)` states that *"whether a steer's target AP is up is not a bound, and is left to the prediction, which counts the flows of a down AP as dead."* The formulation thus relies on the KPI verdict `(S2)` in Eq. (6) to reject any candidate action that steers clients onto a down AP:
     $$\text{accept} \iff \left(\forall k : \delta_k \ge -5\right) \lor \left(\exists k : \delta_k > 5\right).$$
     However, a mathematical contradiction arises from the interaction of Eq. (4) and the disjunctive ($\lor$) acceptance rule in Eq. (6). In Eq. (4), Jain's fairness index $J = \frac{(\sum_i n_i)^2}{N \sum_i n_i^2}$ is computed exclusively over *"the $N$ APs that are up."* When clients are steered from an overloaded operational AP to a down AP, those clients are subtracted from the overloaded AP's client count $n_i$, while the down target AP is excluded from the sum in Eq. (4). Consequently, shedding clients onto a dead AP equalizes $n_i$ across the $N$ surviving operational APs and increases $J$. For example, if three surviving up APs have client counts $(10, 5, 5)$ ($J = 20^2 / (3 \times 150) = 0.889$) and a candidate steer moves $3$ clients from the first AP to a down fourth AP, the surviving up APs have counts $(7, 5, 5)$ ($J = 17^2 / (3 \times 99) = 0.973$), yielding $\delta_J = +9.5\% > 5\%$. Because the second clause of Eq. (6) is connected via a logical disjunction and places no floor on how severely other KPIs regress, $\delta_J > 5\%$ satisfies Eq. (6) by itself and causes the verifier to **accept** steering clients onto the dead AP, even though the twin predicts their flows will become completely dead ($0\text{ Mbit/s}$ throughput, $100\%$ loss, and $1000\text{ ms}$ timeout latency).
   - **Suggested Improvement:** To ensure the safety invariant holds mathematically, enforce target-AP liveness ($\text{up}(\text{target}(a_t)) = 1$) directly as a hard domain constraint in $\mathcal{A}(s_t)$ under `(S1)` and couple the second clause of Eq. (6) conjunctively ($\land$) with a bounded non-regression guard and noise floors matching `(S4)`.

2. **[I2.2] Non-Cancellation Across Saturation Boundaries, Singularity Near $100\%$ Loss, and Fallback Mismatch in Video Demand Reconstruction ($\lambda_f$) (Subsection IV-A, Eqs. 2, 3, and 6)**
   - **Potential Concern:** In Subsection IV-A (page 3, right column), the forward loss model for an inelastic video flow is $\ell_f = \min\{1, 1 - x_f/\lambda_f + b\}$ with base loss $b = 0.2\%$, while the offered demand $\lambda_f$ of a measured video flow is reconstructed from telemetry as its received rate $x_f^{\text{meas}}$ divided by $1 - \ell_f^{\text{meas}}$, falling back to the class's configured rate ($1.0\text{ Mbit/s}$) when $x_f^{\text{meas}} = 0$ or $\ell_f^{\text{meas}} = 1$. While the text notes that omitting $b$ and leaving the ratio uncapped creates an offset carried by both the simulated no-action baseline and the prediction in Eq. (6), three mathematical issues arise because the simulator mapping $\mathbf{k} = f(s, a)$ in Eqs. (2) and (3) is piecewise-bounded and nonlinear in $\lambda_f$:
     1. *Asymmetric clipping across saturation regimes:* When a source AP is congested in the no-action baseline ($\sum_{f \in q} \lambda_f > R_q$), Eq. (2) clips the simulated baseline throughput at the fair share $\theta_q$ (or $R_q$), completely masking any upward inflation in $\lambda_f$. When simulating a candidate action that relieves congestion (e.g., steering clients to an underloaded target AP or changing channels so $R_q$ rises), $\lambda_f$ is no longer clipped at the source AP's $\theta_q$. If $\ell_f^{\text{meas}}$ was close to $100\%$ on the congested source AP (so that $x_f^{\text{meas}} / (1 - \ell_f^{\text{meas}})$ spiked well above the physical source rate before jumping discontinuously back to $1.0\text{ Mbit/s}$ at $\ell_f^{\text{meas}} = 1$), the inflated $\lambda_f$ either unclips to artificially inflate post-action throughput $\hat{x}_f = \lambda_f$ (if $R_q \ge \lambda_f$) or falsely exceeds the target AP's residual capacity $R_q$ (if $1.0\text{ Mbit/s} \le R_q < \lambda_f$), predicting continued saturation, crowding out co-located flows in Eq. (2), and inflating target-AP queue load $\rho_q$ in Eq. (3).
     2. *Fallback rate mismatch with scenario source rates:* Subsection III-A (page 2) specifies that the actual configured video source rates in the three disruption scenarios are $0.4\text{ Mbit/s}$ (flash crowd and co-channel interference) and $0.6\text{ Mbit/s}$ (AP failure). Falling back to $1.0\text{ Mbit/s}$ whenever $x_f^{\text{meas}} = 0$ or $\ell_f^{\text{meas}} = 1$ (such as when `ap2` goes down or before a flow is measured) overstates the true source demand by $67\%$ to $150\%$.
     3. *Forward–inverse mismatch:* Inverting the forward relation $\ell_f = 1 - x_f/\lambda_f + b$ (for $\ell_f < 1$) yields $\lambda_f = \frac{x_f}{1 - \ell_f + b}$ rather than $\frac{x_f}{1 - \ell_f}$.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Reconstruct video demand by clamping at the flow's actual configured scenario rate $\lambda_f^{\text{conf}}$ (cut to any active rate limit) and aligning the denominator with the forward loss formula, e.g., $\lambda_f = \min\left\{\lambda_f^{\text{conf}},\, \frac{x_f^{\text{meas}}}{\max(\epsilon, 1 - \ell_f^{\text{meas}} + b)}\right\}$, eliminating the discontinuity at $\ell_f^{\text{meas}} = 1$ and preventing source congestion from distorting target-AP predictions.

3. **[I2.3] Unspecified Loss Model $\ell_f$ for Web Flows, Insensitivity to Rate Limits, and Scale Distortion in Mean Throughput $T$ (Subsection IV-A, Pages 3–4)**
   - **Potential Concern:** Subsection IV-A (page 3, right column to page 4, left column) defines how web flows participate in capacity sharing and KPI aggregation, raising three mathematical and modeling issues:
     1. *Missing definition of $\ell_f$ for web flows:* The text explicitly defines per-flow packet loss $\ell_f$ for inelastic video flows ($\ell_f = \min\{1, 1 - x_f/\lambda_f + b\}$) and for elastic bulk TCP flows ($\ell_f = b = 0.2\%$), and states that the verdict's mean loss $\bar{\ell}$ is the mean of $\ell_f$ over the compared flows. However, it never specifies the mathematical expression for $\ell_f$ on web flows (which run on all 20 stations in every scenario and are included in every verdict cohort).
     2. *Insensitivity and sign inversion under rate-limit actions:* While a web flow loads its queue in Eq. (2) and $\rho_q$ (Eq. 3) with its configured fetch demand ($\lambda_f = 0.08\text{ Mbit/s}$, cut to its rate limit), its reported throughput in $T$ is modeled as a single isolated page-fetch rate $0.8(C_i - B_q)$, where $B_q$ is the non-web traffic served in its own and higher-priority queues. Because $0.8(C_i - B_q)$ does not depend on the web flow's allocated share $x_f$ or rate-limited demand $\lambda_f$, simulating a throughput cap on a web flow (which Section V compiles onto *"every flow in scope"* from LLM throughput-cap policies) does not reduce its predicted throughput in $T$. Worse, if the policy simultaneously rate-limits non-web flows in the same or higher queues on that AP, $B_q$ decreases and $0.8(C_i - B_q)$ *increases*, causing the twin to predict a throughput gain for the rate-limited web flow. It is also unstated whether $0.8(C_i - B_q)$ is clamped at zero if higher-priority traffic exhausts $C_i$.
     3. *Scale distortion in composite mean throughput $T$:* Whereas Section VII-C excludes web flows from offline throughput validation because their probe measures single-fetch burst rates rather than flow rates, web flows are included in the unweighted arithmetic mean $T$ in Eq. (6) and `(S4)`. Because each web flow contributes up to $0.8 C_i = 3.68\text{ Mbit/s}$ to $T$ ($46\times$ its offered demand $\lambda_f = 0.08\text{ Mbit/s}$ and $3.7\text{--}9.2\times$ a video flow's rate) and is not divided among concurrent web stations on the same AP, $\delta_T$ in Eq. (6) weights idle residual capacity $C_i - B_q$ multiplied by the number of web flows far more heavily than actual carried traffic $\sum_f x_f$.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Explicitly state the formula for $\ell_f$ on web flows so the equations are self-contained, clamp reported web throughput by both zero and any active rate limit ($\min\{\text{rate\_limit}_f, \max(0, 0.8(C_i - B_q))\}$), and either exclude burst fetch rates from the flow-rate mean $T$ in Eq. (6) or weight $T$ by sustained flow rates $x_f$.

4. **[I2.4] Priority-Queue Starvation, Asymptotic vs. Unit-Load Saturation, and Station-Level Shadowing in the Queueing Delay Surrogate (Subsection IV-A, Eqs. 2–3 & Fig. 3)**
   - **Potential Concern:** Subsection IV-A (page 4, left column) models the delay of queue $q$ on AP $i$ via the M/M/1/K mean queue length formula $d_q = d_0 + \sigma L(\rho_q, K)$ with $\rho_q = \sum_{q' \preceq q} \sum_{f \in q'} \lambda_f / C_i$, $d_0 = 0.8\text{ ms}$, $\sigma = 2.5\text{ ms}$, and $K = 6$, and defines $L_{95}$ as the 95th percentile of video flow latencies under the rule that *"all flows of a station report the delay $d_q$ of the highest-priority queue in which the station has a flow on its AP."* Beyond the simplifications already noted by the authors (that $d_q$ is capped at $d_0 + \sigma K = 15.8\text{ ms} < 50\text{ ms}$, that $\rho_q$ uses offered demand $\lambda_f$ rather than throttled TCP rates $x_f$, and that $\sigma$ does not grow as $C_i$ shrinks), three mathematical properties of this formulation warrant attention:
     1. *Disconnect from strict-priority residual capacity $R_q$:* By Little's Law, converting mean queue length $L(\rho_q, K)$ into waiting time requires scaling by the mean service time $1/\mu \propto 1/R_q$ available to queue $q$ in Eq. (2) (or $1/C_i$ for the aggregate link). Under Eq. (3), even if higher-priority traffic consumes $100\%$ of $C_i$ so that a lower-priority queue is completely starved of service ($R_q = 0$, implying unbounded delay or timeout), its predicted delay remains bounded at $15.8\text{ ms}$. Moreover, promoting all flows on an AP from `best_effort` to `priority` leaves $\rho_{\text{priority}}$ equal to the prior $\rho_{\text{best\_effort}}$, predicting $\delta_{L_{95}} = 0$.
     2. *Asymptotic vs. unit-load saturation calibration:* The text states that *"$\sigma$ was fitted to the delay plateau measured at saturation and $K$ bounds the queue, so the predicted delay stays below $d_0 + \sigma K = 15.8\text{ ms}$."* However, in the finite-buffer M/M/1/K formula $L(\rho_q, K)$, the upper limit $K = 6$ is only approached asymptotically as $\rho_q \to \infty$; at the onset of link saturation ($\rho_q = 1$), $L(1, K) = K/2 = 3$, yielding $d_q(1) = 0.8 + 2.5 \times 3 = 8.3\text{ ms}$ (and at $\rho_q = 2$, $L(2, 6) \approx 5.06$ gives $13.4\text{ ms}$, as shown in Fig. 3, right). Equating the measured saturation plateau to $d_0 + \sigma K$ rather than $d_0 + \sigma(K/2)$ causes the surrogate to underpredict delay by nearly a factor of two near $\rho_q = 1$.
     3. *Station-level priority shadowing under QoS actions:* Because all flows on a station inherit the delay $d_q$ of that station's highest-priority active queue, if an LLM-compiled QoS action promotes a station's low-demand `web` flow ($\lambda_f = 0.08\text{ Mbit/s}$) into the `priority` queue while its `video` flow remains in the congested `best_effort` queue, the twin assigns the low delay $d_{\text{priority}}$ to the station's `video` flow when computing $L_{95}$, even though the video packets still traverse `best_effort` (whose cumulative load $\rho_{\text{best\_effort}}$ is unchanged).
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Since the twin tracks per-flow queue assignments when simulating actions, assign each flow $f$ the delay $d_{q(f)}$ of its own queue $q(f)$ when computing $L_{95}$, and scale the service-time coefficient inversely with available capacity (e.g., $\sigma_{q,i} = \sigma C / \max(\epsilon, R_{q,i})$ or $\sigma_i = \sigma C / C_i$) while calibrating $K$ (or adding a starvation penalty when $R_q \to 0$) so saturation delay can exceed the $50\text{ ms}$ threshold in Eq. (5).

5. **[I2.5] Disconnect Between the Control Objective $T_{\text{rec}}$ (Eq. 5), Joint Action-Set Formulation, and Singleton Verification (Subsection IV-B & Section V, Pages 4–5)**
   - **Potential Concern:** Subsection IV-B (pages 4–5) formulates the control problem at each 5 s tick $t$ as choosing a set of actions $a_t \in \mathcal{A}(s_t)$ to minimize $T_{\text{rec}}$ (Eq. 5) subject to joint verification $\hat{\mathbf{k}} = f(s_t, a_t)$ in Eq. (6), where $T_{\text{rec}}$ requires $\mathcal{U}(\tau) < 0.8$ on *"the affected AP"* and network-wide video latency $L_{95}(\tau) \le 50\text{ ms}$ for 15 s. Two structural mismatches arise between this formulation and the verification mechanism:
     1. *Joint formulation vs. stateless singleton verification and concurrent watch attribution:* While Subsection IV-B formulates $a_t \in \mathcal{A}(s_t)$ and $\hat{\mathbf{k}} = f(s_t, a_t)$ over an action set $a_t$, Algorithm 1 (page 5, right column, lines 7–14) allows up to 3 heuristic proposals to be verified and applied in a single 5 s tick, but verifies each proposal $p \in P$ individually as a singleton (`f(s, {p})`) against the unmodified initial tick state $s$ without updating $s$ after an accepted proposal is applied or queued. When `PROPOSE(s)` emits multiple steering proposals in the same tick (e.g., splitting shed clients across multiple underloaded target APs) or proposes both client steering and a channel change for the same overloaded AP, subsequent proposals in that tick are verified against stale pre-action load assumptions. Furthermore, when multiple actions are applied in the same tick, each starts an independent 30 s watch over virtually the same measurement window, making it impossible to attribute a subsequent KPI regression to a specific offending action during rollback.
     2. *Ambiguity in verdict cohort definition and single-AP utilization check:* Subsection IV-B (page 5, top left) defines the verdict cohort as *"the flows whose simulated KPIs the action changes (for a steer, those on the source and the target AP, whose loads both change; for a channel change, those on the AP and on every AP sharing its old or new channel; $J$ is network-wide)."* Conditioning cohort membership on whether simulated KPIs numerically change is ambiguous when a target AP has spare capacity (so its existing flows' simulated throughput $x_f = \lambda_f$ and loss $\ell_f = b$ remain constant even though queue load $\rho_q$ changes) or when evaluating QoS-queue and rate-limit actions (whose structural scopes are omitted from the parenthetical). Furthermore, because Eq. (5) checks $\mathcal{U}(\tau) < 0.8$ only on the single initially affected AP rather than requiring $\max_i \mathcal{U}_i(\tau) < 0.8$ across all up APs, and $\mathcal{U}$ is omitted from Eq. (6), an action that sheds load from the affected AP but pushes a target AP above $0.8$ utilization is neither blocked by Eq. (6) nor penalized by Eq. (5).
   - **Suggested Improvement:** In Algorithm 1, either restrict execution to at most 1 heuristic action per tick (since watch windows already serialize subsequent ticks and cannot isolate concurrent regressions) or update the simulated state sequentially ($s \leftarrow \text{apply}(s, p)$) as proposals are accepted within a tick (or verify the batch jointly, as done for LLM policies), include predicted target-AP utilization $\hat{\mathcal{U}}_i < 0.8$ in the verifier check, and define the verdict cohort explicitly by the structural scope of each of the seven action types rather than by whether simulated KPIs numerically change.

### [Control-Loop Methodology, LLM Intent Pipeline, and Experimental Design] (Pages: 5-6)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **[I3.1] Unchecked Preconditions, Stale Operator Approvals, and Confounded Attribution in the Watch-and-Rollback Mechanism (Section IV-B (S3)–(S4), Page 5; Section V, Algorithm 1, Page 5):**
   - *Potential Concern:* The safety enforcement specifications (S3) and (S4) (Page 5, left column) and Algorithm 1 (line 1) state that approved queued actions are applied without re-verification against a later state, and that for 30 s after an action is applied (ignoring the first 10 s), the live KPIs of the compared flows are monitored; if any KPI regresses by more than 10% and beyond its noise floor, "the action is rolled back, without checking (S1) again, so the rollback assumes the topology did not change during the watch." In a dynamic wireless environment subject to AP failures and traffic surges, three interacting vulnerabilities arise from this design:
     1. *Blind Rollback Without Liveness or Bound Verification:* Reversing a client-steering action without checking target-AP liveness or re-verifying (S1) in the current state $s_t$ can actively push stations back onto a dead AP if the original source AP failed during the 30 s watch window. As reported at the bottom of Page 6 (Section VII-A), this exact interaction caused all 3 failed rollbacks in the AP-failure scenario, stranding stations without a working AP and driving their video ping probe latency to the 1000 ms timeout for the remainder of the run.
     2. *Confounded Regression Attribution and Loop Lockout:* Because the 30 s watch window evaluates raw live KPI changes unconditionally against the pre-action baseline, any scheduled disruption or startup traffic burst occurring within that 30 s window is misattributed to the action under watch, triggering false-positive rollbacks. Furthermore, because Algorithm 1 (lines 2–4) blocks any new heuristic proposals while an earlier change is still being watched, a watch window coinciding with an AP failure locks out the controller from reacting to the new fault until the watch expires.
     3. *Stale Verification of Operator-Queued Actions:* Rule (S3) (Page 5, left column) specifies that "an approved action is applied as verified, not re-verified against a later state." Because network load and client associations evolve while an action awaits operator approval, applying a queued action without re-verifying it against current telemetry $s_t$ bypasses the guarantee that the action remains safe at the moment of execution.
   - *Suggested Improvement:* Modify Algorithm 1 (line 1) and rule (S4) to require every rollback action to pass a liveness and hard-bound check—specifically verifying that the rollback target AP is currently `up` and within capacity/signal bounds (S1) in $s_t$—before execution, aborting the rollback if the target AP is down. Additionally, terminate an active watch early (without rolling back onto a failed node) and release the control loop when telemetry detects a verifiable infrastructure state transition (`up` $\to$ `down`), and require any operator-approved action under (S3) to be re-verified against the latest state $s_t$ immediately prior to execution.

2. **[I3.2] Data Leakage in Calibration/Evaluation Seed Reuse and LLM Prompt Refinement (Section VI, "Scenarios and seeds," Page 6; Section VII-C, Pages 9–10; Figs. 15–17, Page 12):**
   - *Potential Concern:* Section VI discloses two practices in the experimental setup that compromise strict train/validation/test separation:
     1. *Reuse of Calibration Seeds in the Evaluation Campaign:* The twin's parameters ($C, d_0, \sigma, K$), per-class demands, the forecaster, the Isolation Forest anomaly detector (seed 42), and the watch step's 95th-percentile natural-worsening noise floors (S4, Page 5) are calibrated on a 12-run dataset generated with random seeds 42 (training), 43 (validation), and 44 (test). The 27-run closed-loop evaluation campaign reuses the exact same three seeds (42, 43, and 44). Consequently, every evaluation run repeats the identical seeded station mobility trajectories, traffic arrival patterns, and disruption dynamics of the calibration dataset up to the first controller action—and for the baseline controller V1 (which never acts), its evaluation runs are exact duplicates of the calibration runs (with seeds 42 and 43 repeating the training and validation trajectories used to fit the system and noise floors). Only seed 44 represents a strictly held-out test realization, meaning that the test-split evaluations of the Isolation Forest detector (Fig. 16) and Holt's forecaster (Fig. 17) rely on a single held-out run per scenario (seed 44), and evaluating on $n=3$ seeds where two are in-sample calibration seeds risks optimistic bias in the calibrated thresholds and noise floors.
     2. *In-Sample Prompt Revision on the 30-Intent Test Set:* For the offline LLM evaluation (H3), the system prompt (containing schema vocabulary mapping rules and five worked examples) "was revised once after a first run on the same 30 intents, so the set is not held out from the prompt's design." Refining prompt mapping rules after inspecting errors on the exact 30-intent evaluation set (`genai/eval/intents.jsonl`) introduces test-set leakage, making the reported 30/30 exact-match accuracy for the cloud model (`gpt-oss:120b-cloud`) an in-sample result rather than an unbiased measure of generalization to unseen operator phrasings.
   - *Suggested Improvement:* Execute the closed-loop evaluation campaign on a disjoint set of fresh random seeds (e.g., 5 to 10 seeds not used during parameter calibration, Isolation Forest training, or noise-floor estimation). For the LLM intent pipeline, split the intents into a development set for prompt tuning and a strictly held-out test set (including paraphrased, compound, or ambiguous formulations) evaluated without further prompt modifications.

3. **[I3.3] Divergence Between Verifier Scope, Recovery Criterion Scope, and Evaluation Cohort Scope, and Lack of a Client-Roaming Baseline (Section I; Section IV-B, Page 5; Section VI, "Metrics," Page 6; Section VII-A, Pages 6–7; Table II, Page 7):**
   - *Potential Concern:* Across Pages 5–7, three different flow/AP scopes are used to evaluate and gate network performance:
     1. *Verifier Acceptance Rule (Eq. (6), Page 5):* Evaluates relative changes $\delta_k$ only over the flows whose simulated KPIs the candidate action alters (source and target APs for a steer; old/new channel APs for a channel change; network-wide APs for client-count fairness $J$), omitting AP utilization $\mathcal{U}$ entirely, lacking a noise floor for near-zero baseline loss, and allowing a client-count fairness gain ($\delta_J > 5\%$) to pass an action on its own so long as no single KPI degrades by more than 5%.
     2. *Recovery Criterion Scope ($T_{\text{rec}}$, Eq. (5), referenced in Section VI, Page 6):* Evaluates utilization $\mathcal{U}(\tau) < 0.8$ on the single most-utilized surviving AP in the 30 s after $t_d$, while evaluating 95th-percentile latency $L_{95}(\tau) \le 50\text{ ms}$ across *every* video flow in the entire network.
     3. *Evaluation Cohort Scope (Section VI, "Metrics," Page 6 and Table II, Page 7):* Measures mean throughput, mean loss, and $L_{95}$ exclusively for the "affected flows," defined as stations associated with the event AP in the 30 s *before* the disruption $t_d$, fixed by station identity for the rest of the run (note also that in the flash-crowd scenario, video flows on `ap1` only start at $t_d = 210\text{ s}$ as noted in Fig. 7's caption, so clarifying that the cohort is fixed by the *set of stations* on the event AP prior to $t_d$, including video flows initiated by those stations at $t_d$, avoids ambiguity).

     Because these scopes differ, controller actions and baseline behaviors can affect network-wide recovery and cohort KPIs in opposing ways. First, omitting $\mathcal{U}$ from Eq. (6) means the verifier does not directly check the primary utilization condition ($\mathcal{U}(\tau) < 0.8$) required for recovery. Second, in the AP-failure scenario (Table II), the evaluation cohort tracks the stations originally on the failed `ap2`, whereas $\mathcal{U}(\tau)$ tracks the busiest *surviving* AP (`ap1`, `ap3`, or `ap4`). Under V1, `ap2`'s stations automatically re-associate to surviving APs and achieve higher mean throughput ($0.64$ vs. $0.58\text{ Mbit/s}$), lower loss ($4.8\%$ vs. $18.8\%$), and much lower p95 latency ($18\text{ ms}$ vs. $672\text{ ms}$) than V3 on the affected-flows cohort, yet V1 is scored as "never recovered" ($\ge 360\text{ s}$, 3/3 unrecovered) solely because accommodating the displaced stations keeps a surviving AP's utilization $\mathcal{U}(t) \ge 0.8$—a binding metric that is not shown as a column in Table II. Conversely, under V2 and V3, when a failed rollback strands a station on the dead `ap2`, that station no longer loads the surviving APs (helping $\mathcal{U}(\tau)$ drop below 0.8), while its 1000 ms timeout latency prevents global $L_{95}(\tau)$ recovery and skews the three-seed arithmetic mean latency (e.g., averaging $\{1000, 24, 21\}\text{ ms}$ to $348\text{ ms}$). Finally, because V1 lacks native IEEE 802.11k/v/r client roaming, it never recovers within the 600 s horizon in any of its 9 runs, forcing **H1** to rely entirely on right-censored recovery caps (390, 360, and 420 s).
   - *Suggested Improvement:* Explicitly align or tabulate how the three scopes relate in Section VI, include source and target AP utilizations $\mathcal{U}$ in the verifier check, include a column in Table II for the mean post-disruption utilization $\mathcal{U}(t)$ of the bottleneck AP (and report the fraction of stranded stations alongside medians/ranges so bimodal timeout outliers do not distort continuous latency means), and consider evaluating a baseline with standard 802.11k/v/r client roaming (or relaxing the per-AP utilization threshold when total AP count drops after an AP failure) so that H1 does not rely entirely on right-censored recovery times.
